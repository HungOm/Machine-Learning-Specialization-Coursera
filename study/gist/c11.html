C1 · Week 1 · the whole week at once · 12 min

The gist — Introduction to Machine Learning

Thirteen lessons, one loop. This page is the week with the lessons removed — what goes in, what happens to it, what comes out, and which part runs again.

What this page is for

The 13 lesson pages for this week each teach one idea properly. That is the right shape for meeting an idea and the wrong shape for seeing how the ideas join — so this page is only the joins. Nothing here is new: if a claim on this page is not already in one of those lessons, it is a bug.

Read it after the week for consolidation, or before it as a map you will recognise on the way through. Both work; reading it instead of the week does not.

Pick a formula with a couple of unknown numbers in it. Write down a score that says how wrong those numbers are. Then keep nudging them in whichever direction makes the score fall. That is the entire week — and every algorithm in the rest of the specialization is a variation on those three moves.

⛓The pieces, in the order they hand to each other

The pieces, in the order they hand to each other
  1. 1The model

    A straight line with two knobs on it. w tilts the line; b slides it up and down.

    two unknown numbers, and that is the whole model
    fw,b(x)the prediction for one house = wx + b
    the same thing in code
    f = w * x + b

    where it goes wrongDropping b nails the line to the origin — it would insist a house of zero size is worth exactly nothing, and then bend the slope to compensate.

    ↓a predicted price for every house. Useless on its own, until you can say how wrong it is.

  2. 2The cost

    Squash all m misses down to a single number, so that two candidate lines can be compared.

    the average squared miss, halved
    J(w,b)how bad this line is = 12maverage, and halveΣadd over every house (f(x(i)) − y(i)this house’s miss)2
    the same thing in code
    cost = np.mean((f - y) ** 2) / 2

    where it goes wrongThe square is not neutral. A single miss of 10 costs the same as a hundred misses of 1, so one strange house can drag the whole line towards itself.

    ↓one number. The question becomes: which way should w and b move to make that number smaller?

  3. 3The gradient

    The slope of the cost with respect to each knob — steepness and direction in one quantity.

    same sum, twice — the w version carries an extra x∂J∂w = 1mΣ(f(x(i)) − y(i))x(i)∂J∂b = 1mΣ(f(x(i)) − y(i))
    the same thing in code
    err   = f - y
dj_dw = np.mean(err * x)
dj_db = np.mean(err)

    where it goes wrongThe extra x on the w slope is not a typo and not optional: it says a miss on a large house is stronger evidence that the slope is wrong. The bias has no such factor, because b affects every house equally.

    ↓two numbers saying which way is downhill — but not how far to walk.

  4. 4The update

    Walk downhill. Both knobs move, each by its own slope times one shared step size.

    minus, because the slope points uphill and you want the other way
    w := w − αhow big a step∂J∂wb := b − αthe same step size∂J∂b
    the same thing in code
    w = w - alpha * dj_dw
b = b - alpha * dj_db

    where it goes wrongBoth updates must use the old w and b. Overwrite w first and the b update is computed on a line that no longer exists — the classic Week 1 bug, and it does not crash, it just quietly converges to the wrong answer.

🔢The same pass, on numbers you can check

The same thing with numbers small enough to check

Three houses: sizes 1, 2, 3 thousand square feet, prices 3, 5, 8 hundred thousand. Start at w = 0 and b = 0, with α = 0.1. Here is one trip round the loop, in full.

predictions f0, 0, 0w = 0 and b = 0, so every house is predicted to be worth nothing
misses f − y-3, -5, -8all negative — every prediction is too low
cost J16.3333the average of those misses squared, halved
∂J/∂w-12.3333each miss weighted by that house’s size, averaged
∂J/∂b-5.3333the same misses, unweighted, averaged
new w1.23330 − 0.1 × (-12.3333). Two minuses: the step goes up
new b0.53330 − 0.1 × (-5.3333)
cost after one step3.2848down from 16.3333. That fall is the only evidence that any of this works

Run the same three lines 2,000 times and the cost settles at 0.0278, with w = 2.5 and b = 0.333. Nothing else happens. There is no second idea in Week 1 — only this, repeated.

🌀The two loops people confuse

The one thing to remember

There are two different repetitions in this week and they are easy to mistake for each other, because both are written with a Σ or a for.

The inner one runs over the m houses and it happens inside a single step. Its job is to produce two numbers, the gradients. It is finished before the knobs move at all.

The outer one runs over the steps — iteration 1, 2, 3, … Its job is to move the knobs a little each time.

So one full trip round the outer loop looks at every house exactly once. If you find yourself updating w inside the loop over houses, you have merged the two, and you have accidentally invented a different algorithm (a real one — stochastic gradient descent — but not this one, and it will not match the lesson’s numbers).

🔡Every symbol this week, in one table

SymbolSay it out loudWhat it actually is
x“x”The input. One number per house this week: its size.
y“y”The true answer from the data — what the house actually sold for.
ŷ“y hat”The prediction. Plain y is truth, y-hat is a guess.
m“m”How many training examples there are.
x(i)“x superscript i”The i-th example. Round brackets always mean “which example”, never a power.
w“w”, weight or slopeHow much the price rises per unit of size.
b“b”, bias or interceptThe prediction when x is zero; slides the line vertically.
f“f of x”The model itself. Older material calls it h, for hypothesis.
J“J of w, b”The cost — one number saying how badly this line fits.
α“alpha”The learning rate: how far to step each iteration.
∂“partial dee”A derivative when there is more than one variable — the slope in one direction, others held still.
:=“becomes”Assignment, not a claim of equality. w := w − … means replace w.

🚧What this week deliberately cannot do yet

Common trap

Knowing the edges of the week is what stops the next one feeling arbitrary.

One input only. Size, and nothing else. Bedrooms and age are Week 2.

Straight lines only. The model literally cannot bend. Curves arrive in Week 2 as feature engineering, not as a new algorithm.

Numbers out, never categories. “How much?” is answerable; “is this spam?” is not, because a straight line runs off to ±∞ and a probability may not. That is Week 3, and it changes exactly two things: a squashing function on the output and a different cost.

Every example, every step. With a million houses each step reads all million. Real systems do not, and that is a later refinement — not a correction to anything here.

Say the week back, with the page shut

  1. What makes a problem supervised, and which column of the table makes it so.
  2. What the model is, in one line, and what each of its two numbers does to the line.
  3. Why a cost function has to squash m misses into a single number.
  4. Why the misses are squared, and one consequence of that choice.
  5. What a gradient is, in plain words, without saying the word derivative.
  6. Why the w gradient carries an extra x and the b gradient does not.
  7. Why the update subtracts the gradient.
  8. What α controls, and what going too large and too small each look like.
  9. Why both knobs must be updated from the old values.
  10. Which of the two loops runs over houses and which runs over steps.
