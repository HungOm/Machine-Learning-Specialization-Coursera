The gist — Advice for Applying ML

Seventeen lessons and no new algorithm in any of them. This is the week about what to DO when the model is bad — and it is probably the most useful week here.

What this page is for

The 17 lesson pages for this week each teach one idea properly. That is the right shape for meeting an idea and the wrong shape for seeing how the ideas join — so this page is only the joins. Nothing here is new: if a claim on this page is not already in one of those lessons, it is a bug.

Read it after the week for consolidation, or before it as a map you will recognise on the way through. Both work; reading it instead of the week does not.

Every other week teaches you a model. This one teaches you the loop you run around models: measure two numbers, read which of two problems you have, apply one of two sets of fixes, repeat. Getting that diagnosis right is worth more than any architecture choice.

🖼The week in one picture

🎯The central diagnostic

The one thing to remember

Jtrain tells you about bias. The gap between Jtrain and Jcv tells you about variance.

Both bad and close together → underfitting; the model is too simple. Training excellent and the gap large → overfitting; the model memorised. Both can be true at once, and then you need both fixes.

You cannot tell these apart from the training score alone — which is the entire reason a validation set exists.

the six fixes, and which problem each one is for
get more training examplesvariancethe best fix, and the most expensive
try a smaller set of featuresvarianceless flexible
increase λvarianceless flexible
get additional featuresbiasmore flexible
add polynomial featuresbiasmore flexible
decrease λbiasmore flexible
How to never get this backwardsNothing here needs memorising. Every variance fix makes the model less flexible; every bias fix makes it more flexible. Work out which direction you need and the list sorts itself.

💸The most expensive mistake in applied ML

Common trap

“Get more data” appears on one of the two lists.

More data does nothing for high bias. If the model is too simple to capture the pattern, ten times as many examples will be fitted equally badly. And it is the most expensive item on either list — months and money, in a way that changing λ is not.

Teams routinely spend a quarter collecting data for a model that was never going to benefit, and a learning curve would have said so on day one:

  HIGH BIAS                      HIGH VARIANCE
  err                            err
   |  J_cv  ______                |  J_cv \___
   |  J_tr  ______   both flat    |            \___  still falling
   |                              |
   |........... baseline          |  J_tr ____/------
   +--------------- m             +--------------- m
   small gap, both high           big gap, cv still dropping
   -> more data will NOT help     -> more data WILL help
And note J_train RISES with m. Fitting 1,000 points is harder than fitting 10 — a climbing training error is healthy, not broken.

📏Why one number is never enough

The same thing with numbers small enough to check

On a dataset that is 0.5% positive, here is a model with no inputs and no parameters at all.

print("healthy")99.5%accuracy
people it helps0it catches nobody
precisionundefinedit never predicts positive
recall0.00of everyone who was ill, it found none

On skewed data, accuracy measures how rare the positive class is, not how good your model is. Report precision, recall and F1 — and be most suspicious when the headline number is most impressive.

why F1 uses the harmonic mean
P = 1.00, R = 0.01→F1 = 0.0198the harmonic mean — correctly damning
P = 1.00, R = 0.01→ordinary mean = 0.505which would look respectable
The point of F1A harmonic mean sits close to the smaller of the two. F1 refuses to be impressed by a model that is excellent at one half and useless at the other — and an ordinary average would call that model average.

🔍The unglamorous thing that is worth the most

The pieces, in the order they hand to each other
  1. 1Error analysis

    Read the misclassified examples. Actually read them.

    the same thing in code
    wrong = X_cv[preds != y_cv]     # then sample ~100 and READ them

    where it goes wrongLet the categories overlap — one example can be in three. And work on the biggest one that is also tractable: the largest category is worthless if you have no idea how to fix it.

    ↓a ranked list of what is actually wrong, rather than opinions.

  2. 2Data augmentation

    Make new training examples by distorting the ones you have.

    where it goes wrongOne rule decides every case: the distortion must be representative of what really happens. Café noise on speech, yes. Random per-pixel noise on clean scans, no — a scanner never produces that. Mirror-flipping handwritten digits, no — a mirrored 2 is not a 2, and you are teaching it something false.

    ↓more data, if you could not collect any.

  3. 3Transfer learning

    Start from somebody else's trained network instead of from random numbers.

    where it goes wrongReplace only the last layer — everything before it learned generic structure. Freeze the body for a tiny dataset; fine-tune everything for a larger one, and drop the learning rate when you unfreeze, or one large step destroys what somebody spent a fortune learning.

⚖️Two things the data cannot decide for you

The one thing to remember

The threshold. Raise it and precision rises while recall falls — use that when a false alarm is expensive. Lower it and the reverse — use that when a miss is much worse. The right answer depends on what an action costs, which is a question about the world, not about the model.

Whether 92.4% is good. Measure performance per subgroup. Aggregate accuracy hides subgroup failure by construction: a group that is 6% of your data can be served terribly and move the headline by under a point. And removing the sensitive attribute does not fix it — the model finds proxies and reconstructs the group anyway.

🕳Three ways a split can silently lie

Common trap

Splitting after sorting — the halves are different populations. Shuffle first, unless it is a time series, where you must split by time.

Duplicate entities — the same house, patient or user in both sets. The model recognises rather than generalises.

Scaling before splitting — μ and σ saw the test set.

What they share: your test score comes out too good, you ship confidently, and the model underperforms in production for reasons nobody can reproduce. A suspiciously excellent test score deserves suspicion, not celebration.
