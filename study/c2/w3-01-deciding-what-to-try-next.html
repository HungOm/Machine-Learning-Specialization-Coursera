9.1Deciding what to try next

The most valuable week of the course starts with an uncomfortable fact: most ML time is wasted on the wrong fix.

❓Before you read

Your model's predictions are bad. You could get more data, add features, remove features, change λ, or make the network bigger. How would you decide which — without trying all five?

I have committed to an answer

Watch for why guessing costs months. This whole chapter is about replacing the guess with a diagnosis.

🎈The idea, in plain words

In plain English

Your bike won’t go. You could pump the tyres, oil the chain, tighten the brakes, straighten the wheel, or buy a whole new bike. Each takes an afternoon.

A bad mechanic tries them in order until something works — five wasted afternoons.

A good mechanic spins the wheel first and listens. Ten seconds, and now they know it’s the brake rubbing. That ten-second check is called a diagnostic, and this whole week is about the two diagnostics that matter in machine learning.

🔧If you have never seen a formula

A car that will not start. One mechanic changes the battery, then the plugs, then the starter motor, working through parts by price until something happens. Another puts a meter on it, finds no spark, and replaces one component.

Both know engines. Only the second one is diagnosing, and the difference is not knowledge — it is having a test that narrows the possibilities before spending money.

🌉If you know a neighbouring field

Anyone who has debugged a production system knows the discipline: form a hypothesis, find the cheapest measurement that distinguishes it from the alternatives, then act.

Machine learning is unusually bad at encouraging this, because every option — more data, more features, more layers, more regularisation — sounds plausible in isolation and takes days to try. Hence a whole week on measurement.

👁One thing to picture

A list of six things you could do next, and an arrow pointing at only one of them.

The list is short and everyone can write it. The arrow is the entire content of this week, and the thing that produces the arrow is two numbers: training error and cross-validation error.

⚖️Where this really costs something

This is the week that separates people who can run a model from people who can improve one, and it is worth more career-wise than any algorithm in the specialization.

Andrew's own framing is blunt: teams routinely spend six months collecting data that a ten-minute diagnostic would have shown was not the bottleneck. The cost of guessing is measured in quarters.

So the list below is not a menu of remedies. It is a list of things that each fix a different disease.

Notice the asymmetry: the most expensive item on the list — collecting more data — is useless against high bias. Teams routinely spend a quarter collecting data for a model that was never going to benefit from it.

The one thing to remember

A diagnostic is a test you run to find out what is actually wrong, so you stop choosing your next move by instinct. Diagnostics take time to implement, and they still pay for themselves several times over.

🔤The words, decoded

SymbolSay it out loudWhat it actually is
diagnostic“a diagnostic”A test you run to find out why a model is bad, rather than guessing. This whole week is diagnostics.
bias“bias”, as in underfittingThe model is too simple to fit even the training data. Nothing to do with fairness — different word, same spelling.
variance“variance”, as in overfittingThe model fits the training data and not much else.
baseline“baseline”What a reasonable alternative achieves — usually a human. Without it, an error rate means nothing.
Say it out loud

“Bias” appears twice in this week with two unrelated meanings. Say what each one is, and why the collision is worth being careful about.

said it — now check

Bias as underfitting is a property of a model that is too simple to capture the pattern — a purely technical quantity, measured by the gap from the baseline to training error. Bias as unfairness is systematically worse outcomes for a group of people.

They share a word and nothing else. The collision matters because “we reduced bias” is a sentence that can mean two entirely different pieces of work, and in a report to non-specialists it will be read the second way. Say which one you mean.

✅Check yourself

Your model has 0.1% error on training data and 30% on new data. Is more data likely to help?

Yes — that is the signature of high variance, and more data is a valid fix for it. (The next few lessons make this precise.)

Your model has 25% error on training data and 26% on new data. More data?

No. It cannot even fit the data it already has. More of the same will not help. This is high bias.
