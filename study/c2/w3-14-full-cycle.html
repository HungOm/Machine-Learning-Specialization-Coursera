Modelling is maybe 20% of the work. Here is the other 80%, including the parts that only bite after launch.

Watch for how much of the work sits after the modelling — and for the part that never ends.

🎈The idea, in plain words

In plain English

Baking a cake isn’t just mixing. It’s deciding what cake to make, buying ingredients, mixing, baking, and checking it hasn’t gone stale a week later.

Machine learning projects have the same shape, and the last part — the “is it still good?” part — is the one people forget. A model that was excellent in March can be quietly useless by September, because the world moved and the model didn’t.

🔧If you have never seen a formula

A restaurant is not a recipe. Somebody has to buy the produce, cost the dish, get it past environmental health, train the staff, and deal with it when the supplier changes.

The cooking is the part everyone pictures and a small fraction of the work. A deployed model is the same shape, and the modelling is the part that gets the attention.

🌉If you know a neighbouring field

This is MLOps, and if you have shipped any software you know most of it already: versioning, monitoring, rollback, on-call.

The genuinely new part is that the model degrades without anyone touching it, because the world changes underneath it. Software does not usually rot on its own; models do, and it is called drift.

👁One thing to picture

A loop with four stations — scope → collect data → train → deploy — and, crucially, an arrow from deploy back to collect.

That return arrow is the point. Deployment produces new data, which trains the next model. A system in production is a data source, not a finished object.

⚖️Where this really costs something

The industry rule of thumb is that modelling is 10–20% of the effort of a deployed system, and data plumbing plus monitoring is most of the rest.

The failure that costs most is silent drift: a fraud model trained on last year's fraud, still reporting good offline metrics on last year's held-out data, quietly missing this year's patterns. Monitoring the inputs, not just the accuracy, is what catches it.

The one thing to remember

The arrows that go backwards are the ones that consume your quarter. Error analysis sends you back to data collection. Production feedback sends you back to training. Plan for the loop, not for a straight line.

🚀What deployment actually involves

A typical setup: your model sits behind an inference server; the mobile app calls an API; predictions come back. Then the parts nobody mentions in tutorials:

  • Monitoring — track the input distribution, not just uptime. A prediction service can be 100% available and 100% wrong.
  • Data drift — the world changes. New slang breaks a spam filter; a new phone camera breaks a vision model.
  • Feedback loops — your model influences the data it later trains on. A recommender that never shows an item never learns whether people wanted it.
  • Rollback — you will ship a bad model one day. Be able to undo it in minutes.

The field name for this is MLOps. It is a whole discipline, and it is where most ML value is actually won or lost.

🕳Traps

Common trap

Treating deployment as the finish line. It is the start of the maintenance phase, which lasts as long as the product does.

Common trap

No monitoring of inputs. Accuracy metrics need labels, which arrive late or never. Watching the input distribution shift gives you an early warning that needs no labels at all.

Say it out loud

Monitoring accuracy needs labels, which arrive late or never. Monitoring the input distribution needs none. Say why that makes input monitoring the more useful alarm.

said it — now check

Because it fires first. If your users' inputs shift — a new device, a new market, a changed upstream field — you can see that today, whereas the accuracy consequence only becomes visible when labels eventually arrive, which for churn or fraud can be months.

It cannot tell you how much harm the shift is doing, so it is an early warning rather than a measurement. That is still the difference between noticing drift in a week and noticing it in a quarter.

✅Check yourself

Your deployed spam filter's accuracy drops over six months with no code change. Why?

Data drift. Spammers changed tactics; the distribution moved away from your training data. The fix is retraining on recent data, and monitoring that would have caught it sooner.

Why monitor input distributions rather than just accuracy?

Because true labels in production are delayed or unavailable. Input drift is measurable immediately and predicts trouble before accuracy reports arrive.
