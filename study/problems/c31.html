Problems — Clustering and anomaly detection

Two unsupervised algorithms that look unrelated and share a shape: fit something simple to the data, then measure how well each point matches. Both are small enough to run by hand, and doing so is worth it.

How to use this page

Work each one on paper, with the solution shut. Open the hint only after you are genuinely stuck, and the solution only after you have written something down — even a wrong something. Reading a worked solution you have not attempted feels like learning and is not.

Then grade yourself honestly. A missed problem pulls that lesson’s review cards forward to tomorrow and shows up in weak spots.

3stretchlocal optima↗ lesson
On the same six points, two initialisations give different final answers:
A starts at points 1 and 4 → clusters {1,2,3} and {4,5,6}, J = 1.778
B starts at points 1 and 3 → clusters {1,2} and {3,4,5,6}, J = 1.313
Which is better, what does that prove about k-means, and what is the standard fix?
worked solution
  1. Lower cost is betterB, at 1.313, beats A's 1.778
  2. Both are converged — neither can improve by another stepboth are local optima
  3. So the answer k-means gives depends on where it startednot a global optimum
  4. Fix: run it many times from different random initialisations
    your turntypically 50–1000 times
  5. Keep the run with the lowest J
    your turnthe cost function is the referee
answerB is better (J = 1.313 versus 1.778). This proves k-means finds a local optimum that depends on initialisation. The fix is to run it many times from random starts and keep the lowest-cost result.
What this is really testingThis is why the cost function matters even though k-means has no gradient descent: J is how you choose between runs. Without it you would have no way to tell A from B.
4corek-means cost↗ lesson
The k-means cost is J = (1/m) Σ ‖x(i) − μc(i)‖². Explain why this cost can never increase during either step of the algorithm, taking each step in turn.
worked solution
  1. Assignment step: each point is moved to the nearest centroidits own term can only shrink or stay equal
  2. Centroids do not move during this stepso no other term changes
  3. Move step: each centroid becomes the mean of its pointsthe mean is exactly the point that minimises the sum of squared distances
  4. So each cluster's contribution can only shrink or stay equal
    your turnassignments do not change during this step
  5. J is bounded below by 0 and never rises
    your turnit must converge
answerThe assignment step moves each point to its nearest centroid, which can only reduce that point's term. The move step sets each centroid to its cluster's mean, and the mean is precisely the minimiser of summed squared distance. Neither step can increase J, and J ≥ 0, so it converges.
What this is really testingIf your implementation ever shows J rising, you have a bug — usually assignments and centroids being updated in the wrong order or from mixed states.
5corechoosing k↗ lesson
You run k-means for k = 1…8 and record J: [52.1, 24.3, 11.8, 9.9, 8.7, 7.8, 7.1, 6.5]. (a) Why can J never increase as k grows? (b) Where is the “elbow”? (c) Why is the elbow method often unhelpful in practice?
worked solution
  1. (a) more centroids means every point can be at least as close as beforeJ is non-increasing in k, by construction
  2. (b) look for where the drop suddenly flattens52 → 24 → 12 → 9.9: the big falls stop after k = 3
  3. (c) real curves are usually smooth with no sharp cornerthe elbow is a matter of opinion
  4. Better: choose k by what the clusters are for
    your turn3 T-shirt sizes vs 5 is a business decision, not a maths one
answer(a) Adding a centroid can only bring points closer, so J never rises. (b) About k = 3. (c) Real J curves are usually smooth, so different people see the elbow in different places — and the right k is usually decided by the downstream use, not the plot.
What this is really testingNote you can never choose k by minimising J, since J is always lowest at k = m, where every point is its own cluster and the cost is exactly zero.
6coreGaussian↗ lesson
A feature has values [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Compute μ and σ² (dividing by m), then compute p(x = 7) using p(x) = (1/√(2πσ²)) e−(x−μ)²/(2σ²).
worked solution
  1. Mean(3+4+5+6+7) ÷ 5 = 25 ÷ 5 = 5
  2. Squared deviations4, 1, 0, 1, 4
  3. Variance10 ÷ 5 = 2, so σ = 1.414
  4. Exponent at x = 7−(7−5)² ÷ (2×2) = −4 ÷ 4 = −1
  5. Front factor
    your turn1 ÷ √(2π×2) = 1 ÷ √12.566 = 1 ÷ 3.545 = 0.282
  6. Multiply
    your turn0.282 × e⁻¹ = 0.282 × 0.368
answerμ = 5, σ² = 2, p(7) ≈ 0.1038
What this is really testingCompare with p(5) ≈ 0.282 at the mean. Two standard deviations out is already about 2.7× less likely — and that ratio is what anomaly detection thresholds.
7stretchanomaly detection↗ lesson
Two features are fitted as μ₁ = 5, σ₁² = 2 and μ₂ = 10, σ₂² = 4. With ε = 0.001, classify (5, 10), (8, 10) and (5, 16). Use p₁(5)=0.2821, p₁(8)=0.0297 and p₂(10)=0.1995, p₂(16)=0.0022.
stuck? one hint
The features are assumed independent, so the joint probability is the product. Compare the product against ε, not each feature separately.
worked solution
  1. (5, 10) — both at their means0.2821 × 0.1995 = 0.0563
  2. (8, 10) — first feature 2σ out0.0297 × 0.1995 = 0.0059
  3. (5, 16) — second feature 3σ out0.2821 × 0.0022 = 0.00063
  4. Compare each with ε = 0.001
    your turn0.0563 > ε · 0.0059 > ε · 0.00063 < ε
answer(5,10) normal · (8,10) normal · (5,16) anomaly
What this is really testingNotice no single feature is impossible on its own. Anomaly detection multiplies, so several mildly odd values — or one very odd one — can push the product below ε.
8coreanomaly vs supervised↗ lesson
Choose anomaly detection or supervised learning for each, and give the reason:
(a) 20 known fraud cases, 10,000 legitimate, and new fraud tactics appear monthly
(b) 6,000 spam and 6,000 legitimate emails, spam looks much like last year's
(c) finding never-before-seen faults in aircraft engines
(d) predicting which of 5 known machine faults has occurred
worked solution
  1. (a) very few positives, and future positives will look different from past onesanomaly detection
  2. (b) plenty of both classes, and future positives resemble past onessupervised
  3. (c) “never before seen” is the definition of the anomaly caseanomaly detection
  4. (d) 5 known, well-represented categories
    your turnsupervised, multi-class
answer(a) anomaly (b) supervised (c) anomaly (d) supervised
What this is really testingThe deciding question is not how many positives you have — it is whether the next positive will look like the ones you have already seen. If not, no supervised model can learn it.
9stretchchoosing features↗ lesson
A feature's histogram is strongly right-skewed with a long tail. What transformation does the course suggest and why? Give the two forms of the transformation.
worked solution
  1. The algorithm assumes each feature is roughly Gaussiana skewed feature breaks that assumption
  2. A log compresses large values much more than small onesthe long tail is pulled in
  3. Plain log fails on zeros, since log(0) = −∞so add a small constant
  4. Two usual forms
    your turnnp.log(x + c) or x**p for a small power p
  5. Check the histogram afterwards and tune c or p until it looks bell-shaped
    your turnthis is a manual, visual step
answerTake log(x + c) (or xp for a small power). The log squashes the long tail so the feature becomes roughly Gaussian, which is what the algorithm assumes. The constant c exists to survive zeros.
What this is really testingThis is one of the few genuinely manual steps in the whole specialization — you look at a histogram, choose a transform, and look again.
10stretchtuning epsilon↗ lesson
You have 10,000 normal engines and 20 known faulty ones. Describe how to split this data and how to choose ε, and explain why the faulty examples must not go in the training set.
worked solution
  1. Training set: normal examples only — this is where μ and σ² are fittede.g. 6,000 normal
  2. Cross-validation: 2,000 normal + 10 faultyused to choose ε
  3. Test: 2,000 normal + 10 faultyused once, at the end
  4. Sweep ε over many values, compute F1 on the cross-validation set each time
    your turnnot accuracy — the data is extremely skewed
  5. Faulty examples must not train μ and σ² because they would widen the fitted Gaussian
    your turnwhich makes the anomalies look normal
answerFit μ and σ² on normal examples only; put the faulty ones in the cross-validation and test sets. Sweep ε and pick the value maximising F1 on cross-validation. Including faults in training would inflate σ² and make the very things you are hunting look ordinary.
What this is really testingThis is the one place where a labelled example is deliberately withheld from training. The model is meant to describe “normal”, so anything abnormal would corrupt the description.

12corek-means edge case↗ lesson
During k-means a centroid ends up with no points assigned to it. What breaks, and what are the two standard responses?
worked solution
  1. The move step computes the mean of that cluster's pointsthe mean of an empty set is 0/0
  2. Response 1: drop that centroidyou now have k − 1 clusters
  3. Response 2: re-initialise it at a random data pointkeeps k intact
  4. The course's default is to drop it; keeping k matters if k was chosen for an external reason
    your turnlike a fixed number of T-shirt sizes
answerThe move step divides by zero, since the cluster has no points to average. Either eliminate that centroid (leaving k − 1 clusters) or re-initialise it at a randomly chosen data point.
What this is really testingThis is much more common than it sounds when k is large or the initialisation is unlucky — it is one of the reasons k-means++ initialisation exists.
