Problems — The Maths You Actually Need

Sixteen problems, deliberately shuffled so you have to work out what kind of problem each one is before you can solve it. Paper and pencil — the point is to produce the maths, not recognise it.

How to use this page

Work each one on paper, with the solution shut. Open the hint only after you are genuinely stuck, and the solution only after you have written something down — even a wrong something. Reading a worked solution you have not attempted feels like learning and is not.

Then grade yourself honestly. A missed problem pulls that lesson’s review cards forward to tomorrow and shows up in weak spots.

1warm-upfunctions↗ lesson
A function is defined as f(x) = 3x − 4. Work out f(0), f(2) and f(−1).
worked solution
In plain wordsA function is a rule: whatever number you feed it, the rule does the exact same thing to it, every time. Here the rule is “triple it, then take away 4”.
  1. Put 0 where every x isf(0) = 3·0 − 4 = 0 − 4
  2. Put 2 where every x isf(2) = 3·2 − 4 = 6 − 4
  3. Put −1 where every x is. Watch the sign: 3 × (−1) = −3
    your turnf(−1) = 3·(−1) − 4 = −3 − 4
answerf(0) = −4, f(2) = 2, f(−1) = −7
A second way to see itRead f(0) off a graph of y = 3x − 4 instead of computing it: it is exactly where the line crosses the vertical axis. Same answer, no arithmetic.
What this is really testingA function is a machine, and the letter in the brackets is whatever you feed it. Everything later — the model, the cost, the sigmoid — is this one move repeated.
2warm-upslope↗ lesson
A straight line passes through (1, 7) and (5, 19). Find its slope, then say what the slope means in words.
stuck? one hint
Slope is rise over run: how much y changed, divided by how much x changed.
worked solution
In plain wordsYou are asking how steep a line is: compare how far up two points are apart against how far across they are apart.
  1. Rise — how far up19 − 7 = 12
  2. Run — how far across5 − 1 = 4
  3. Divide
    your turn12 ÷ 4 = 3
answerslope = 3. In words: every time x goes up by 1, y goes up by 3.
A second way to see itTest a third point the slope predicts. From (1, 7), stepping 2 more to the right should add 2 × 3 = 6 to y, landing at (3, 13). Check that point is on the line and you have confirmed the slope independently, with no division involved.
What this is really testingIn ML the slope of the cost curve is the gradient, and the gradient is the whole of gradient descent. If you can read a slope you can read a gradient.
3coresigma↗ lesson
Given x = [4, 1, 6, 9], evaluate Σi=14 (xi − 5)2.
stuck? one hint
Σ says: do the thing inside once for every i, then add up the results. Subtract first, square second, add last.
worked solution
In plain wordsΣ means “do this to every number in the list, then add up the results”. Here “this” is: subtract 5, then square.
  1. i = 1(4 − 5)² = (−1)² = 1
  2. i = 2(1 − 5)² = (−4)² = 16
  3. i = 3(6 − 5)² = (1)² = 1
  4. i = 4
    your turn(9 − 5)² = (4)² = 16
  5. Now add the four results
    your turn1 + 16 + 1 + 16
answer34
A second way to see it5 is exactly the mean of [4, 1, 6, 9] (4+1+6+9 = 20, ÷ 4 = 5) — so this sum is exactly the numerator of the variance from the Mean and Variance lesson. Divide your answer by 4 and you get the variance of this list: 34 ÷ 4 = 8.5.
What this is really testingThis is the squared-error cost with the 5 playing the part of the prediction. You have just computed the numerator of J(w,b) by hand.
4corederivatives↗ lesson
Differentiate each one with respect to x:
(a) f(x) = x2   (b) f(x) = 5x   (c) f(x) = 7   (d) f(x) = 3x2 − 4x + 9
stuck? one hint
One rule does all four: the derivative of xn is n·xn⁻¹. A plain number has derivative 0, because a flat line has no slope.
worked solution
In plain wordsDifferentiating asks: nudge x by a tiny amount right now — how much does the output change, per unit of nudge? The one rule covers all four: bring the power down as a multiplier, then drop the power by one.
  1. (a) power rule: bring the 2 down, drop the power by one2x1 = 2x
  2. (b) 5x is 5·x¹ → bring down the 15·1·x0 = 5
  3. (c) a constant never changes, so its slope is flat0
  4. (d) differentiate term by term
    your turn3·2x − 4·1 + 0 = 6x − 4
answer(a) 2x   (b) 5   (c) 0   (d) 6x − 4
A second way to see itCheck (a) the numerical way from Lesson 5: nudge x = 3 by h = 0.001. ((3.001)² − 3²) ÷ 0.001 = 6.001 — matching 2x = 2(3) = 6 with no differentiation rule needed at all.
What this is really testingEvery gradient in this specialization is one of these three rules applied to a slightly longer expression. Nothing harder ever appears.
5coredot product↗ lesson
With a = [2, 0, 3] and b = [1, 5, −2], compute a · b. Then compute b · a and say what you notice.
worked solution
In plain wordsA dot product pairs two lists position by position, multiplies each pair, and adds everything into one number.
  1. Multiply matching positions2×1 = 2  ·  0×5 = 0  ·  3×(−2) = −6
  2. Add the three products2 + 0 + (−6)
  3. Now the other way round
    your turn1×2 + 5×0 + (−2)×3 = 2 + 0 − 6
answera · b = −4 and b · a = −4 — the dot product does not care about the order.
A second way to see itNotice both directions multiplied the exact same three pairs of numbers, just written in a different order — multiplication does not care which factor comes first, so adding those same three products twice was always going to give the same total.
What this is really testingThe dot product turns two lists into one number. That is exactly what a neuron does to its inputs and weights before it adds b.
6warm-uplogarithms↗ lesson
Without a calculator: (a) log10(1000)   (b) log2(8)   (c) ln(1)   (d) log(0.001) [base 10]
stuck? one hint
Read log as a question: “what power do I raise the base to, to get this?”
worked solution
In plain wordsA logarithm asks a “what power” question. log₁₀(1000) is asking: 10 to what power gives 1000?
  1. (a) 10 to what power is 1000? 10³ = 10003
  2. (b) 2 to what power is 8? 2³ = 83
  3. (c) e to what power is 1? Anything to the power 0 is 10
  4. (d) 0.001 = 10⁻³
    your turn−3
answer(a) 3   (b) 3   (c) 0   (d) −3
A second way to see itCheck (d) a different way: 0.001 = 1÷1000, and log of a reciprocal is always minus the log of the original — so log(0.001) = −log(1000) = −3, matching part (a) with the sign flipped.
What this is really testingLogistic loss is built out of logs. The one fact that matters there: log of something near 1 is near 0, and log of something near 0 is a huge negative number.
7corematrix multiplication↗ lesson
Multiply A = [[1, 2], [3, 4]] by B = [[5, 6], [7, 8]]. Before you compute anything, write down the shape of the answer.
stuck? one hint
Shape first: (2×2) times (2×2) gives (2×2). Each output cell is a row of A dotted with a column of B.
worked solution
In plain wordsEach cell of the answer is one ordinary dot product: one row of the first matrix paired against one column of the second.
  1. Shapes: (2×2)·(2×2). The inner 2s match, so it is legal; the outer 2s give the answer shaperesult is 2×2
  2. Row 1 of A · column 1 of B1×5 + 2×7 = 5 + 14 = 19
  3. Row 1 of A · column 2 of B1×6 + 2×8 = 6 + 16 = 22
  4. Row 2 of A · column 1 of B
    your turn3×5 + 4×7 = 15 + 28 = 43
  5. Row 2 of A · column 2 of B
    your turn3×6 + 4×8 = 18 + 32 = 50
answerAB = [[19, 22], [43, 50]]
A second way to see itRead the first cell through the Dot Product lesson’s shopping-list picture: row 1 of A is [1, 2], column 1 of B is [5, 7] — a 2-item basket priced at [5, 7], total bill 1×5 + 2×7 = 19. Same arithmetic, a completely different way of seeing it.
What this is really testingEvery forward pass through a layer is this. Get the shape rule into your fingers and half of all neural-network bugs disappear.
8coremean & variance↗ lesson
For the data [2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9] find the mean, then the variance (divide by n), then the standard deviation.
worked solution
In plain wordsMean is just “add them all up, divide by how many there are” — the ordinary average. Variance measures how spread out the numbers are around that average.
  1. Add them up2+4+4+4+5+5+7+9 = 40
  2. Divide by n = 8μ = 40 ÷ 8 = 5
  3. Distance from the mean, each one squared9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4, 16
  4. Add those and divide by 8
    your turn32 ÷ 8 = 4
  5. Standard deviation is the square root of the variance
    your turn√4 = 2
answerμ = 5, σ² = 4, σ = 2
A second way to see itBefore squaring, check the deviations themselves: −3, −1, −1, −1, 0, 0, 2, 4 add up to exactly zero. Deviations from the true mean always cancel — if yours had not summed to zero, that would be the moment to go back and check the mean.
What this is really testingFeature scaling is exactly this, done to a column and then used to rewrite it. Z-score scaling is (x − μ) ÷ σ and nothing else.
9stretchpartial derivatives↗ lesson
For f(w, b) = w2b + 3b, find ∂f/∂w and ∂f/∂b, then evaluate both at (w, b) = (2, 5).
stuck? one hint
For ∂/∂w, freeze b — treat it as if it were the number 5 the whole way through. For ∂/∂b, freeze w.
worked solution
In plain wordsFinding ∂f/∂w means: pretend b is frozen at some fixed number, then differentiate as if w were the only thing that could ever move.
  1. ∂/∂w: b is frozen, so w²b is “a constant times w²”, and 3b is just a constant∂f/∂w = 2wb + 0 = 2wb
  2. ∂/∂b: w is frozen, so w²b is “a constant times b”, and 3b differentiates to 3∂f/∂b = w² + 3
  3. Evaluate the first at w = 2, b = 52·2·5 = 20
  4. Evaluate the second at w = 2
    your turn2² + 3 = 4 + 3 = 7
answer∂f/∂w = 2wb and ∂f/∂b = w² + 3; at (2, 5) they are 20 and 7.
A second way to see itLand on ∂f/∂b = 7 a different way: freeze w at its actual value, 2, before differentiating. f(2, b) = 2²b + 3b = 4b + 3b = 7b — an ordinary one-variable function whose derivative is just 7. No partial notation required at all.
What this is really testingGradient descent updates w and b at the same time using exactly this pair of numbers. The freezing move is the whole idea of a partial derivative.
10coreprobability↗ lesson
A model says an email is spam with probability P = 0.8. (a) What is the probability it is not spam? (b) If you classify anything above 0.5 as spam, what does this model say? (c) Two independent emails both get 0.8 — what is the chance both are spam?
worked solution
In plain wordsAll the possible outcomes must add up to 1, and independent events multiply rather than add.
  1. (a) Probabilities of all the outcomes add to 11 − 0.8 = 0.2
  2. (b) 0.8 is above the 0.5 thresholdpredict spam
  3. (c) Independent events multiply
    your turn0.8 × 0.8 = 0.64
answer(a) 0.2   (b) spam   (c) 0.64
A second way to see it0.8 × 0.8 asks: what fraction of the time would both of two independent, 80%-likely events happen? Multiplying two numbers under 1 always shrinks the answer — so 0.64 being smaller than 0.8 is exactly what should happen, not a mistake.
What this is really testingLogistic regression outputs a probability, not a label. The threshold is a separate decision you make afterwards — and you are allowed to move it.
11warm-uptranspose↗ lesson
Write down AT where A = [[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]]. What shape is A, and what shape is AT?
worked solution
In plain wordsTransposing tips the grid on its side: whatever sat in row i, column j moves to row j, column i. Nothing is calculated — only where each number sits changes.
  1. A has 2 rows and 3 columnsA is 2×3
  2. Transposing swaps rows and columns: row 1 of A becomes column 1 of Aᵀ[1, 2, 3] → first column
  3. Row 2 of A becomes column 2 of Aᵀ
    your turn[4, 5, 6] → second column
answerAT = [[1, 4], [2, 5], [3, 6]]  — A is 2×3 and AT is 3×2.
A second way to see itConfirm the shape with no grid-drawing at all: A is (2 rows, 3 columns), and transposing always swaps the two numbers round, so AT must be (3 rows, 2 columns) — you know the shape of the answer before moving a single number.
What this is really testingTranspose exists almost entirely to make shapes line up so a multiplication is legal. When NumPy shouts about shapes, a transpose is usually the fix.
12coreexponentials↗ lesson
The sigmoid is g(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z). Compute g(0), g(2), and g(−2). Use e−2 ≈ 0.135 and e2 ≈ 7.389.
stuck? one hint
Do the exponent first, then the 1 +, then the division. Nothing else.
worked solution
In plain wordsThe sigmoid squashes any number into somewhere between 0 and 1: work out the exponent first, add 1, then divide 1 by that.
  1. z = 0: e⁰ = 1g(0) = 1 / (1 + 1) = 1/2 = 0.5
  2. z = 2: the exponent is −2, so e⁻² ≈ 0.135g(2) = 1 / (1 + 0.135) = 1 / 1.135 ≈ 0.881
  3. z = −2: the exponent is +2, so e² ≈ 7.389g(−2) = 1 / (1 + 7.389) = 1 / 8.389 ≈ 0.119
  4. Notice the pair
    your turn0.881 + 0.119 = 1.000
answerg(0) = 0.5, g(2) ≈ 0.881, g(−2) ≈ 0.119. And g(−z) = 1 − g(z) — sigmoid is symmetric about 0.5.
A second way to see itGet g(−2) with no exponentials at all: g(−z) = 1 − g(z) is always true, so once g(2) ≈ 0.881 is known, g(−2) must be 1 − 0.881 = 0.119 — the pairing rule does the work.
What this is really testingYou will meet g(z) hundreds of times. Knowing g(0) = 0.5 by heart tells you the decision boundary sits where z = 0, which is where wx + b = 0.
13stretchargmax↗ lesson
A softmax layer outputs [0.1, 0.6, 0.05, 0.25] for classes [cat, dog, bird, fox].
(a) What is max? (b) What is argmax? (c) Why does the difference matter?
worked solution
In plain wordsmax picks out the biggest number in the list. argmax picks out where that number sits — its position, not its value.
  1. max asks “what is the biggest value?”0.6
  2. argmax asks “at which position?” Counting from 0index 1
  3. Index 1 in the class list
    your turndog
answer(a) 0.6   (b) 1, i.e. dog   (c) max is the confidence; argmax is the prediction. You report the label from argmax and the confidence from max.
A second way to see itA fast way to catch the classic mix-up: if an answer to argmax looks like a probability (somewhere between 0 and 1), max was accidentally computed instead — argmax must always be a whole-number position.
What this is really testingMixing these up is a real and common bug: printing 0.6 where the class name was wanted, or comparing an index against a probability.
14corevectors↗ lesson
With u = [3, 4] and v = [1, 2], compute u + v, 2u, and the length ‖u‖.
worked solution
In plain wordsAdding vectors combines them position by position. Multiplying by a plain number stretches every entry by that much. Length is Pythagoras, with more terms if the vector is longer.
  1. Adding is position by position[3+1, 4+2] = [4, 6]
  2. Multiplying by a number stretches every entry[2·3, 2·4] = [6, 8]
  3. Length is Pythagoras
    your turn√(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25
answer[4, 6], [6, 8], ‖u‖ = 5
A second way to see it[3, 4] is the well-known 3–4–5 triangle, so ‖u‖ = 5 should look immediately familiar — no calculator required for this particular pair of numbers.
What this is really testing‖w‖² is the thing regularization shrinks. A vector's length is a single number measuring “how big are these weights, overall”.
15stretchnormal distribution↗ lesson
Exam marks are normally distributed with μ = 70 and σ = 10. (a) Between which two marks do about 68% of students fall? (b) About 95%? (c) A student scores 85 — how many standard deviations above the mean is that?
stuck? one hint
The 68–95–99.7 rule: one σ either side holds ~68%, two σ holds ~95%.
worked solution
In plain wordsμ is where the middle of the bell sits. σ is roughly how far a typical value strays from it. The 68–95–99.7 rule says what fraction of everything falls within 1, 2 or 3 of those typical distances.
  1. (a) one σ either side of the mean70 − 10 = 60 and 70 + 10 = 80
  2. (b) two σ either side70 − 20 = 50 and 70 + 20 = 90
  3. (c) that is the z-score: (x − μ) ÷ σ
    your turn(85 − 70) ÷ 10 = 15 ÷ 10
answer(a) 60 to 80   (b) 50 to 90   (c) z = 1.5
A second way to see itCheck part (c) against (a) and (b) rather than trusting the arithmetic alone: a score of 85 sits at z = 1.5, which is between the 68% boundary (z = 1, up to 80) and the 95% boundary (z = 2, up to 90) — so 85 should feel somewhat unusual but nowhere near extreme, exactly where 1.5 sits between 1 and 2.
What this is really testingAnomaly detection in Course 3 flags points with a very low probability under a fitted Gaussian. The z-score is how you say “how unusual is this” in one number.
16stretchΣ + slope together↗ lesson
A model predicts f(x) = 2x + 1. The training data is (1, 3), (2, 5), (3, 8). Compute the cost J = (1/2m) Σ (f(x(i)) − y(i))2.
stuck? one hint
Three columns on paper: prediction, error, error squared. Then sum, then divide by 2m where m = 3.
worked solution
In plain wordsThis is the Course 1 cost function, run on three real houses: for each one, see how wrong the line’s guess was, square that error, add the three up, then average.
  1. Predictionsf(1) = 3, f(2) = 5, f(3) = 7
  2. Errors, prediction minus actual3−3 = 0,   5−5 = 0,   7−8 = −1
  3. Square them0, 0, 1
  4. Sum
    your turn0 + 0 + 1 = 1
  5. Divide by 2m, with m = 3
    your turn1 ÷ 6
answerJ = 1/6 (about 0.167)
A second way to see itThe line predicted the first two houses exactly right — both errors were 0. The entire cost of 1/6 came from missing the third house alone by 1. A perfect model here would have scored J = 0 exactly.
What this is really testingThis is the whole of Course 1 Week 1 in one calculation. If you can do this on paper you can read every cost-function plot in the course.
