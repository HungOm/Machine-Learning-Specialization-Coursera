C4 · Week 4 · 6 problems · 1 warm-up · 3 core · 2 stretch

Problems — Language models

The last set in the specialization. These are less about arithmetic and more about being able to explain a real system precisely — which is the skill that actually transfers.

How to use this page

Work each one on paper, with the solution shut. Open the hint only after you are genuinely stuck, and the solution only after you have written something down — even a wrong something. Reading a worked solution you have not attempted feels like learning and is not.

Then grade yourself honestly. A missed problem pulls that lesson’s review cards forward to tomorrow and shows up in weak spots.

1warm-uploss and perplexity↗ lesson
A model assigns probability 0.20 to the token that actually came next. Compute the loss and the perplexity, and say what the perplexity means in words.
worked solution
In plain wordsTake the negative log of the probability, then raise e to that answer.
  1. Loss−log(0.20) = 1.6094
  2. Perplexitye^1.6094 = 5.0
  3. Note
    your turnperplexity = 1 ÷ probability, when it is a single token
answerLoss 1.609, perplexity 5.0 — the model is about as uncertain as if it were picking uniformly among five tokens.
A second way to see itA uniform guess over a 50,257-token vocabulary would give perplexity 50,257 and loss 10.825.
What this is really testingPerplexity is quoted in papers precisely because “effectively choosing between 5 options” is far more interpretable than “loss 1.6”.
2coretemperature↗ lesson
Three tokens have scores [2, 1, 0]. Compute the sampling probabilities at T = 0.5 and at T = 2.0, and describe the difference.
stuck? one hint
Divide the scores by T first, then softmax.
worked solution
  1. T = 0.5: divide[4, 2, 0]
  2. Exponentiate54.60, 7.39, 1.00 → sum 62.99
  3. Softmax[0.867, 0.117, 0.016]
  4. T = 2.0: divide[1, 0.5, 0]
  5. Exponentiate
    your turn2.718, 1.649, 1.00 → sum 5.367
  6. Softmax
    your turn[0.507, 0.307, 0.186]
answerAt T = 0.5: [0.867, 0.117, 0.016] — sharply focused. At T = 2.0: [0.507, 0.307, 0.186] — much flatter, so unlikely tokens get picked far more often.
A second way to see itThe ordering never changes — only how sharply the leader is favoured. That is why T → 0 becomes argmax.
What this is really testingNot a single model weight differs between these two columns. Temperature is a serving setting, which is why the same model can feel like two different systems.
3corecontext cost↗ lesson
A model has 6 layers and d = 256. Compute its KV cache at a context of 2,048 tokens, in fp16 (2 bytes per number). Then say how the cache and the attention work each scale with length.
stuck? one hint
The cache stores both a key and a value per layer per token.
worked solution
  1. Numbers cached per token per layer2 (one key, one value) × 256 = 512
  2. Across 6 layers6 × 512 = 3,072 numbers per token
  3. Across 2,048 tokens3,072 × 2,048 = 6,291,456 numbers
  4. At 2 bytes each
    your turn12,582,912 bytes
answerAbout 12.6 MB per sequence. The cache grows linearly with length; attention work grows with the square.
A second way to see itDouble the context to 4,096 and the cache doubles to 25 MB while the attention work quadruples.
What this is really testingTwo different scaling laws in one system. Memory is usually what limits how many simultaneous users a machine can serve, which is why so much serving research targets this cache specifically.
4corefailure modes↗ lesson
For each failure, name the mechanism that causes it: (a) confidently stating a false fact, (b) miscounting the letters in a word, (c) losing track of something said earlier in a long conversation.
worked solution
  1. (a) hallucinationthe training objective rewards LIKELY continuations; nothing in it ever distinguished true from plausible
  2. (b) letter countingtokenization — the model receives subword chunks and never sees individual letters
  3. (c) forgetting
    your turnthe context window — anything outside it does not exist at all
answer(a) the next-token objective rewards plausibility, not truth; (b) tokenization hides letters; (c) the fixed context window, beyond which there is simply no memory.
What this is really testingEvery one of these follows from the design rather than from insufficient training — which is why they are predictable, and why scale reduces some of them and removes none.
5stretchRLHF↗ lesson
A freshly pretrained model is asked “What is the capital of France?” and replies “What is the capital of Germany? What is the capital of Spain?”. Explain why this is a reasonable output, and what the two additional training stages each fix.
worked solution
  1. What the model was trained to docontinue text plausibly
  2. In its training dataa question is frequently followed by more questions — a list, a quiz, an exercise sheet
  3. So the continuation is genuinely likelythe model is succeeding at its objective
  4. Stage 2 — supervised fine-tuning
    your turndemonstrations teach the FORMAT of an answer
  5. Stage 3 — RLHF
    your turnhuman rankings teach which answers people actually prefer
answerIt is a perfectly good next-token prediction — questions often precede more questions in real text. Stage 2 teaches the format of answering; stage 3 teaches which answers people prefer, including when to decline.
A second way to see itStage 1 supplies essentially all the capability. Stages 2 and 3 are cheap by comparison and change the entire experience of using the model.
What this is really testingStage 3 is reinforcement learning — the reward is a learned model of human preference and the policy is the language model. Course 3 Week 3 was not a detour.
6stretchsynthesis↗ lesson
Someone claims a language model “understands” the document you gave it. Write the precise, mechanical version of that claim — what is actually computed — and say what part of the claim remains genuinely open.
worked solution
  1. Each token becomes a vectorby embedding lookup, plus a positional encoding
  2. Each blocklets every position read every other, weighted by learned relevance
  3. After 12 or 96 blockseach position's vector is contextualised by the whole window
  4. Those representations support
    your turnaccurate prediction of what comes next
  5. What is not established
    your turnwhether that constitutes understanding in any sense beyond the predictive one
answerMechanically: the model computes, for every token, a representation shaped by every other token in the window, in a way that supports accurate next-token prediction. Whether that amounts to understanding is an open question — the mechanism is what you can state with confidence.
What this is really testingBeing able to give the mechanical account, and to mark clearly where it stops, is a large part of what it means to actually understand these systems rather than to have opinions about them.
