conceptWhat does f(3) mean — and what does it definitely not mean?

“Put 3 into the machine called f.” It is not f multiplied by 3.

A function is a rule where the same input always gives the same output.

A model is a function. Training does not change the function — it changes numbers inside it, which is what the subscript in fw,b(x) is telling you.
In plain English

A vending machine for numbers. Put a coin in, get a chocolate bar out — same coin, same bar, every time.

symbolsay itwhat it means
f“eff”the machine's name. Could be g, h, J, σ — all just names
f(x)“f of x”what comes out. The brackets mean “applied to”, never “times”
f(3)“f of three”put 3 in. If f(x) = 2x+1 the answer is 7

In code: def f(x): return 2*x + 1

Walk me through it

Think of f as the name of a machine, the way kettle is the name of a machine. f(3) means put 3 into the machine called f and see what falls out. The brackets are a doorway, not a multiplication sign.

Say the machine is f(x) = 3x − 4. Then:

  • put in 0 → 3×0 − 4 = −4
  • put in 2 → 3×2 − 4 = 2
  • put in −1 → 3×(−1) − 4 = −7

The one rule that makes it a function: the same thing in always gives the same thing out. Put 2 in a hundred times and you get 2 back a hundred times.

Why this matters later: a trained model is one of these machines. Training never changes the machine — it changes the numbers sitting inside it. That is what the little w, b underneath in fw,b(x) is telling you: same machine, adjustable parts.

in NumPy
def f(x):
    return 3*x - 4
f(0), f(2), f(-1)
→(-4, 2, -7)

formulaSlope — the formula, and the two letters it goes by.
slope = riserun = change in ychange in x

Written m in school maths, w in machine learning. Same thing.

“If I step 1 to the right, how far up do I go?” Positive = uphill, negative = downhill, zero = flat.
In plain English

How steep a line is, as one number: step one to the right, how far up do you go?

symbolsay itwhat it means
rise“the rise”how much it went UP. Negative if it went down
run“the run”how far you went ACROSS
m“em”the school-maths letter for slope
w“double-you”the SAME thing, in machine learning

y = mx + c at school is f(x) = wx + b here. Identical; only the letters changed.

Walk me through it

Slope is one number answering one question: if I step one to the right, how far up do I go?

worked all the way through
  1. 1Pick two points on the line. Say (2, 3) and (6, 11).
  2. 2How far up? 11 − 3 = 8. That is the rise.
  3. 3How far across? 6 − 2 = 4. That is the run.
  4. 4Divide: 8 ÷ 4 = 2. That is the slope.
The pointThis line climbs 2 units up for every 1 across. A slope of 0 is flat. A negative slope goes downhill.

The letters trip people up more than the idea does. At school it was probably m. Here it is w, for weight — same number, different name, because w is about to become a whole list of them.

Everything in gradient descent is this one idea, asked about a hill instead of a line.

in NumPy
(19 - 7) / (5 - 1)      # rise over run
→3.0

conceptWhat is a derivative, and what are the only three things you need to know about it?

The slope of a curve at one exact point — found by taking two points very close together and shrinking the gap to nothing.

  • it is a slope
  • its sign says which way is uphill
  • its size says how steep
f′(x) = limh→0 f(x+h) − f(x)h
You never need to differentiate by hand in this specialization. TensorFlow does it.
In plain English

Slope needs two points and a curve gives you one. So take a second point very close by, then slide it closer and closer. The number it settles on is the derivative.

symbolsay itwhat it means
f′(x)“f prime of x”the derivative. The little dash is all it is
df/dx“dee f by dee x”the same thing, written differently
h“aitch”the tiny gap to the second point
lim h→0“as h goes to zero”“keep shrinking the gap and see what it approaches”

In code: (f(x+h) - f(x)) / h with h about 1e-5. Any smaller and rounding ruins it.

Walk me through it

A slope needs two points. A curve at a single spot only gives you one. So: take a second point very close by, work out the slope between them, then slide the second point closer and closer. The number it settles on is the derivative.

Try it on f(x) = x² at x = 3, with the second point a thousandth away.

measured, with a ruler
up a hair(3.001)² = 9.006001just to the right
down a hair(2.999)² = 8.994001just to the left
rise ÷ run0.012 ÷ 0.002 = 6.0the slope between them

And the calculus rule says the derivative of x² is 2x, which at x = 3 is 6. The measurement and the rule agree.

The pointThat agreement is not a coincidence and it is not just pretty — it is exactly the check the from-scratch files run on every gradient they write. Calculus you did by hand, against arithmetic that needs no calculus. If they match, the calculus was right.
only three things about a derivative matter here: it is a slope, and then
Its sign
says which way is uphill.
Its size
says how steep the hill is.
in NumPy
w, eps = 3.0, 1e-6
((w+eps)**2 - (w-eps)**2) / (2*eps)     # d/dw of w**2 at w=3
→6.0

conceptWhat is a partial derivative, and what does the curly ∂ tell you?

Freeze every variable except one, and a hill becomes an ordinary curve whose slope you already know how to find.

The curly ∂ (rather than a straight d) is purely a notice that other variables exist and are being held still.

Collect all the partials into one list and you have the gradient, ∇J — which points straight uphill. That is why gradient descent subtracts it.
In plain English

You are on a hillside. “How steep?” is incomplete — steep in which direction? Pick one direction, walk only along that line, and you are back to an ordinary slope.

symbolsay itwhat it means
∂“partial” or “curly dee”a curly d. It only means “other variables are being held still”
∂J/∂w“dee J dee w”how much J changes if you nudge w alone
∇J“grad J”all the partials collected into one list — the gradient

In code, “held still” literally means you do not add h to it.

Walk me through it

You are standing on a hillside. Someone asks “how steep is it?” — and the question is incomplete, because it depends which way you face.

A partial derivative answers the fixed-up version: pick one direction, hold everything else still, and measure the steepness along that line only.

Take f(x, y) = x²y at the point x = 2, y = 3.

  • Steepness in the x direction: pretend y is a frozen 3, differentiate x²·3 → 6x → at x = 2 that is 12.
  • Steepness in the y direction: pretend x is a frozen 2, differentiate 4y → 4 → that is 4.

Two numbers, one per direction. The curly ∂ — say it “partial dee” — is the only thing announcing “there are other variables and I am holding them still”.

This is why gradient descent has one slope per weight. A model with a million weights is standing on a hillside with a million directions, asking the same small question a million times.

in NumPy
w, b, eps = 2.0, 5.0, 1e-6
def f(w, b):
    return w**2*b + 3*b
((f(w+eps,b)-f(w-eps,b))/(2*eps), (f(w,b+eps)-f(w,b-eps))/(2*eps))
→(20.0, 7.0)
✎ on paperDraw a hill in 3-D, badly. Slice it once north–south and once east–west. Each slice is a partial: label which letter you froze for each.

formulaΣ — what is it, and how do you read it out loud?
Σi=1m xi

A for loop. Counter underneath, stop on top, thing-to-add beside it.

  • read as: “now do this for every training example, and add up the results”
  • in code: np.sum(x)
Σ(x²) squares each then adds. (Σx)² adds then squares. For [1,2] those are 5 and 9.
In plain English

Writing x₁ + x₂ + … + x₁₀₀₀ is silly, so maths has a shorthand. It is a for loop wearing a hat.

symbolsay itwhat it means
Σ“sum of” (capital sigma)add up what follows
i = 1“i starts at one”the counter and where it begins — underneath
m“up to m”where it stops — on top. Usually the number of examples
xᵢ“x sub i”the thing being added; i changes each time round

In code: np.sum(x), or np.sum(x ** 2) for Σx².

Walk me through it

Σ is a capital Greek S and it stands for Sum. It is a for loop that someone wrote down instead of typing.

Read it in three parts, bottom, top, right:

  • bottom — where the counter starts (i = 1)
  • top — where it stops (m, and it includes m)
  • right — the thing to work out each time round, then add on

So with the list [3, 1, 4]: start at the first, take 3; next, add 1 to get 4; next, add 4 to get 8. Done.

In code that whole symbol is usually a single call: np.sum(x), or x.sum(). Cost functions look frightening mostly because they are a Σ wrapped around something else — and the Σ is never the hard part. Cover it with your thumb, read what is to the right of it, then remember it happens once per example and gets added up.

in NumPy
x = np.array([4., 1, 6, 9])
np.sum((x - 5)**2)
→34.0

formulaΠ — what does it mean, and why does code usually avoid it?

Capital pi: multiply them all together. (Nothing to do with 3.14159 — that is lowercase π.)

log(a × b) = log(a) + log(b)

Multiplying many numbers below 1 underflows to exactly zero, so code computes np.sum(np.log(p)) instead.

The shrinking is the point in anomaly detection: five mild oddities multiply into one genuinely rare event.
In plain English

Sigma's sibling. Same layout, one different instruction: multiply instead of add.

symbolsay itwhat it means
Π“product of” (capital pi)multiply them all together
π“pi” (lowercase)the COMPLETELY different thing: 3.14159
underflow“UNDER-flow”so many small numbers multiplied that the answer rounds to zero

In code: np.prod(p) — but real code writes np.sum(np.log(p)) to stay safe.

Walk me through it

Π is a capital Greek P and it means Product — the same idea as Σ but with multiplying instead of adding.

Now the practical problem. Multiply probabilities together and they collapse: 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.125. Do that a thousand times and the answer is smaller than the smallest number a computer can hold. It becomes 0, and everything after it is nonsense. That failure has a name — underflow.

The escape is a logarithm, which turns multiplying into adding:

  • log(0.125) = −2.0794
  • log(0.5) + log(0.5) + log(0.5) = 3 × (−0.6931) = −2.0794

Same number, and the second route never made a tiny number along the way.

So Π appears in the maths and almost never in the code. Anywhere you would have multiplied a long list of probabilities, real code adds their logs instead. That single swap is why log-likelihood and log loss exist at all.

in NumPy
p = np.array([0.9, 0.8, 0.7])
np.prod(p), np.exp(np.sum(np.log(p)))   # same, but logs do not underflow
→(0.504, 0.504)

formulaWhat is a vector, and how do you find its length?

A list of numbers in order. In ML, almost always one row of your spreadsheet.

‖x‖ = √x12 + x22 + …

np.linalg.norm(x). For [3, 4] it is √(9+16) = 5.

Double bars ‖x‖ for a vector's length; single bars |x| for one number's absolute value.
In plain English

A list of numbers kept in order. Two numbers you can draw as an arrow; four hundred you cannot, and every formula works identically.

symbolsay itwhat it means
x“x vector”the little arrow means “this is a list, not one number”
‖x‖“the norm of x”its LENGTH. Double bars
|x|“absolute value”single bars — a different thing, for single numbers
scalar“SCAY-lar”the word for an ordinary single number
√square rootthe number that, times itself, gives this one. √9 = 3

In code: np.linalg.norm(x). It is just Pythagoras with more terms.

Walk me through it

A vector is a list of numbers with an order: [3, 4]. You can picture it as an arrow from the origin out to the point (3, 4).

Its length is measured with Pythagoras, which is the same rule you would use for a ladder against a wall:

  • square each number: 3² = 9, 4² = 16
  • add them: 9 + 16 = 25
  • square root: √25 = 5

It works the same in any number of dimensions — just keep adding squares. [1, 2, 2]: 1 + 4 + 4 = 9, so the length is 3. You cannot picture seven dimensions and you can still compute the length, which is the whole reason to trust the algebra over the picture.

The double bars, ‖v‖, are said “norm of v”. In code it is np.linalg.norm(v). When a lesson says a model's weights got “big”, this is the number it means.

in NumPy
np.linalg.norm([3., 4])
→5.0

formulaThe dot product — both formulas, and what the answer means.
a · b = Σ aibi = ‖a‖‖b‖ cos θ
  • multiply the pairs, add them up → one number
  • positive = pointing the same way · zero = at right angles · negative = opposed
a @ b, not a * b. The star keeps them separate; the @ collapses them. A neuron needs the collapse.
In plain English

A shopping list and a price list. Pair each item with its price, multiply, add it all up. One number comes out: the total bill.

symbolsay itwhat it means
·“dot”the dot product — includes the adding, unlike ordinary ×
aᵀb“a transpose b”the same thing, written the matrix way
cos θ“cos theta”how much two arrows point the same way
Σcapital sigmaadd all of these up. A for loop
‖the norm of xthe length of a vector. Double bars

In code: a @ b gives 32. a * b gives [4, 10, 18] — a list, not a total.

Walk me through it

The dot product takes two lists and gives back one number: multiply them position by position, then add up the lot.

  1. 1Pair them up by position: a = [1, 2, 3] with b = [4, 5, 6].
  2. 2Multiply each pair: 1×4 = 4, 2×5 = 10, 3×6 = 18.
  3. 3Add them: 4 + 10 + 18 = 32.
[1, 2, 3] · [4, 5, 6]→32two lists in, one number out

The lists must be the same length — there is nothing to pair a leftover with.

two things to take away
It is the prediction step
Features in one list, weights in the other. Dot them and you have the prediction. The whole loop is one character in code: a @ b.
It also measures agreement
Geometrically it says how much two arrows point the same way. Zero means at right angles. That reading is the one PCA and attention use.
in NumPy

a @ b, np.sum(a*b)      # same number, two ways
→(-4.0, -4.0)

formulaThe matrix multiplication shape rule, and the trick for checking it in your head.
(m × n) × (n × p) = (m × p)

Write the shapes side by side. Middles match → legal. The answer is the outer two.

The inner number gets summed away and never appears in the result. And A@B ≠ B@A — usually one of them is not even defined.
In plain English

A test you can do in your head before writing any code: can these two tables be multiplied, and what size will the answer be?

symbolsay itwhat it means
(m × n)“m by n”m rows, n columns. Rows always first
inner numbers—the two in the middle. They MUST match
outer numbers—the two on the ends. They become the answer's shape
≠not equal tothese two are different — the shape rule uses it a lot

In code: A @ B. Write (3×2)(2×4) — middles match, answer is (3×4).

Walk me through it

Matrix multiplication is fussy about sizes, and one trick settles it every time. Write the two shapes side by side and look at the middle pair.

      (4, 2)  @  (2, 3)
          |         |
          +----+----+
               |
        these must MATCH

      (4, 2)  @  (2, 3)
       |                |
       +--------+-------+
                |
        these are the ANSWER  ->  (4, 3)
Inner pair must match. Outer pair is the result.

Read the answer as a sentence: 4 examples, each with 2 features, going into a layer of 3 units, gives 4 examples each with 3 outputs. The shapes are not bookkeeping — they are the meaning.

The pointGet the inner pair wrong and NumPy stops with a shape error. That error is a friend: almost every neural-network bug you will hit is a shape bug, and this trick finds it before you run anything.
in NumPy
A, B = np.zeros((2, 3)), np.zeros((3, 4))
(A @ B).shape          # inner 3s cancel, outer 2 and 4 remain
→(2, 4)
✎ on paperWrite (2, 3) and (3, 4) side by side. Ring the two inner numbers and join them with a line saying must match. Ring the outer two and write the answer's shape.

conceptWhat does transpose do, and why do you keep needing it?

Swaps rows and columns. (2,3) becomes (3,2). The numbers do not change — only where they sit.

You need it for one boring reason: making two shapes line up so a multiplication is defined.

M.T. And it is not reshape — both can give (3,2) from a (2,3), and the numbers land in different places.
In plain English

Tip the spreadsheet on its side. Rows become columns. No number changes value; they just move.

symbolsay itwhat it means
Mᵀ“M transpose”the maths notation — a raised capital T
M.T“M dot tee”the NumPy version. Same thing
view“a view”NumPy does not copy anything — it just changes how it walks the numbers

You reach for it when shapes refuse to match: X @ W.T.

Walk me through it

Transpose tips a table on its side: rows become columns, columns become rows. In code it is one character, M.T.

Start with a 2-row, 3-column table:

  • [[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]] — shape (2, 3)
  • transposed: [[1, 4], [2, 5], [3, 6]] — shape (3, 2)

The first row, 1 2 3, has become the first column. Nothing was calculated; nothing moved except the way you read it.

Why it keeps appearing: it is almost always there to make the shape rule work. You have data as (examples, features) and you need (features, examples) so the inner numbers line up. When you meet X.T @ err in a gradient, that is all the .T is doing — turning the table so the multiplication is legal, and so the sum comes out per-feature instead of per-example.

in NumPy
X = np.array([[1., 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]])
X.T.shape, X.T
→((3, 2), [[1., 4.], [2., 5.], [3., 6.]])

conceptTwo facts about ez that explain why it is everywhere in this specialization.

  • it grows very fast, turning differences in scores into ratios in probability

Why e and not 10? Because the derivative of ex is ex itself — which makes every derivative in the course come out clean.
In plain English

e is a fixed number, about 2.718, like π. “e to the power z” is always positive and grows very fast — and those two facts are the whole reason it appears everywhere.

symbolsay itwhat it means

∂J/∂w = (∂J/∂d)(∂d/∂a)(∂a/∂c)(∂c/∂w)multiply the local slopes along the path
The pointEach node only ever needs to know its own little multiplier — how much its output moves when its input moves. It knows nothing about the network as a whole, and it does not need to.

  2. 2Walk right to left, multiplying by each node's local slope as you pass it.

  2. act in the environment; store each (s, a, R(s), s′) tuple

The trigonometry you actually need

9 terms

This specialization never asks you to do trigonometry. It just uses four or five facts and assumes you have them. Here they are, and nothing beyond them is needed anywhere in the three courses.

Every trig word is a side of this triangle. The angle θ decides which side is called which: opposite is across from it, adjacent is beside it, and the hypotenuse is always the slanted one.

The same three ratios on a circle of radius 1. Walk round the edge and cos θ is how far across you are, sin θ is how far up. That is the whole reason both stay between −1 and 1.

Where it actually shows up

  • The dot product. a · b = ‖a‖‖b‖ cos θ — the lengths are fixed, so the sign of the answer is the sign of cos θ. Positive means roughly the same direction, zero means perpendicular, negative means opposed.
  • Cosine similarity (C3 W2). (a · b) ÷ (‖a‖‖b‖). Dividing by both lengths cancels size out, so only direction is compared. That is why a user who rates everything 5 still counts as similar to one who rates everything 2.

NumPy works in radians. np.cos(90) is not cos 90° — it is cos of 90 radians, which is −0.448. Use np.cos(np.deg2rad(90)), which gives 0 as intended.

Projection — the idea PCA rests on

3 terms

The PCA lesson says “project: z = x · u” and moves on. That one word is doing all the work, and it is the most visual idea in the whole course.

Shine a light straight down onto the line. The shadow of the point x is its projection, and the single number z is how far along the line that shadow falls. Everything at right angles to the line is discarded — and discarding it is the entire point.

PCA picks the line to project onto: the direction the cloud is most spread along. PC2 is chosen at right angles to PC1, so it measures something PC1 cannot see. Keep PC1 only, and every point becomes one number.

termsay itthe formulawhat it gives you
projectionpro-JEC-shunz = x · uone number — how far along u
unit vectorunit vector‖u‖ = 1,   u = v ÷ ‖v‖a direction with no size attached

orthonormalor-tho-NORM-aluᵢ · uⱼ = 0,   ‖uᵢ‖ = 1directions that do not double-count

Why the dot product measures a shadow

The geometric dot product is x · u = ‖x‖ ‖u‖ cos θ. Make u a unit vector and ‖u‖ becomes 1, leaving ‖x‖ cos θ — which is exactly the adjacent side of the right-angled triangle formed by dropping a perpendicular. The shadow.

So “dot with a unit vector” and “measure the shadow” are the same instruction.

Where it actually shows up

  • PCA (C3 W2). z = x · u for each component you keep. 50 features become 2 numbers you can plot.
  • Reconstruction error. Project down and back up; what does not survive the round trip is what you threw away.
  • Every neuron. w · x is a projection too — it asks “how much of this input points along the direction I care about?”
The trap

The direction must have length 1. x · u is only a distance when ‖u‖ = 1. Use a direction of length 2 and every projection comes out twice too large — the number would then be measuring the arrow you chose as well as the point you are looking at. Libraries always hand back unit vectors, which is why this rarely bites in practice and is baffling when it does.

Growth rates — why some methods stop working

2 terms

All three courses say things like “that does not scale” and “about n³” without ever showing what that means. It is not about speed. It is about shape: how fast the work grows when the problem grows.

All three start in the same corner. That is the trap — on small data every method looks fine, and the difference only appears once the problem is big enough to matter.

The same n³ method at four sizes, at roughly a billion operations a second. Ten times the features is a thousand times the wait: one second becomes seventeen minutes.

growthdouble the input and…n = 1,000 costswhere it appears
O(1)nothing changes1looking up one array element
O(n)work doubles1,000one pass over the data — a gradient descent step
O(n²)work × 41,000,000comparing every pair — distances between all points
O(n³)work × 81,000,000,000inverting a matrix — the normal equation

The two claims this explains

  • “Use gradient descent, not the closed form.” (C1 W2) The normal equation inverts an n×n matrix — about n³ work in the number of features. At 10,000 features that is roughly 17 minutes for one fit. Gradient descent is about O(n) per step.
  • “Vectorize.” (C1 W2) This one is different, and worth being precise about: vectorization does not change the growth rate. The loop and np.dot do the same number of multiplications. It is a constant-factor win — often 10–100× — from using the hardware properly. Both lines on the chart have the same shape; one is simply far lower.

The shape of your data decides the cost

A matrix multiply of (m, n) by (n, k) costs about m · n · k multiply-adds. So a batch of 64 examples through a 1,000→500 layer is 64 × 1,000 × 500 = 32 million.

That is why doubling the batch size doubles the cost, but doubling the width of two adjacent layers quadruples it.

The trap

Big-O throws away the constants, and sometimes the constants are what you care about. An O(n) method with a huge constant can lose to an O(n log n) method on every size you will ever run. Big-O tells you what happens eventually; it does not tell you what is faster today. Use it to spot the method that will fall off a cliff, then measure.

Three kinds of average, and when each is wrong

3 terms

“Average” is used loosely across the courses for three different calculations. Twice it matters a great deal which one is meant, and in one of those cases using the wrong one silently breaks a decision tree.

The same two numbers, 0.9 and 0.1, averaged two ways. The ordinary average says 0.50 — respectable. The harmonic mean says 0.18, because one of the two is nearly zero and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

A lopsided split: nine mixed examples and one pure one. Weighting by branch size gives a gain of 0.108. Forgetting the weights gives 0.505 — nearly five times too flattering, and the tree takes the useless split.

averageformula0.9 and 0.1use it when
arithmetic(a + b) ÷ 20.500the values count equally
weighted(w₁a + w₂b) ÷ (w₁ + w₂)depends on wsome values represent more than others
harmonic2ab ÷ (a + b)0.180a single bad score should sink the result

Where each one is load-bearing

  • Information gain (C2 W4) — weighted. The children's entropies are averaged by branch size. Drop the weights and a branch holding one pure example looks like a triumph.
  • F1 (C2 W3) — harmonic. Chosen precisely so that precision 1.0 with recall 0.01 scores 0.0198 rather than 0.505.
  • Cost functions (everywhere) — arithmetic. Every example counts once.
  • Ensembles (C2 W4) — boosting uses a weighted vote; bagging uses a plain one.

An honest caveat

When the branches happen to be the same size, weighting changes nothing. The lecture's cat dataset splits 5 and 5 on ear shape, so its weighted and unweighted answers are identical — both 0.7219.

That is exactly why the mistake survives: it agrees with the correct answer on the worked example everybody checks against, and only diverges on the lopsided splits that occur deeper in a real tree.

The trap

The harmonic mean is not “a stricter average”. It is close to the ordinary average when the two numbers are close — 0.6 and 0.5 give 0.55 and 0.545, a difference of half a percent. It only diverges when they are far apart, and then it follows the smaller one. That is the entire behaviour, and it is why F1 is safe to quote without also quoting precision and recall — though you should quote them anyway.
