C3 · Week 1 · 10 questions · pass mark 80%

Unsupervised Learning

K-means and anomaly detection. The split question and the “which technique” question are the two the graded quiz always asks in some form.

Closed book. No notes, no scrolling back — that is the condition the real one is sat under.
—

  1. the algorithm

    What are the two steps k-means alternates between?

    • Two steps, repeated until nothing moves. Each one cannot increase the cost, which is why it terminates.

    • Random assignment happens once at initialisation, not every round, and computing the cost is not one of the two update steps.

    • That is PCA, which appears in Week 2.

    • That describes hierarchical clustering, a different family entirely.

  2. initialization

    Why run k-means many times from different random initialisations?

    • Two flags landing in the same corner give a genuinely worse clustering, and nothing in the algorithm can escape it. Run it 50–1000 times and keep the lowest cost.

    • Once initialised, both steps are deterministic. Only the starting position is random.

    • That is a reasonable thing to want and is not why the restarts are prescribed here.

    • Restarts compare runs at a fixed k. The cost cannot be used to choose k because it falls automatically as k rises.

    What this is really testingOne run of k-means is a sample, not an answer.

  3. the cost functionselect all that apply

    Which are true of the k-means cost function J?

    • Sometimes called inertia or distortion. It is the quantity both steps reduce.

    • Both steps can only reduce it, which proves the algorithm terminates — and makes a rising J a reliable assertion that you have a bug.

    • J falls automatically as k rises, reaching zero when every point is its own cluster. Choosing k needs the elbow, or a business reason.

    • It is not convex in the assignments, which is exactly why local optima and restarts matter.

    • It can be computed at any iteration, which is what lets you plot it and check it is falling.

  4. choosing k

    Using the elbow method you plot J against k and get a smooth curve with no visible bend. What should you do?

    • Andrew is explicit that often no clear elbow exists. A t-shirt maker choosing between three sizes and five is weighing fit against inventory cost, and that decides it.

    • That is always the largest k you tried, ending with one cluster per point.

    • It clusters fine. The absence of an elbow says there is no single natural number of groups, not that grouping is impossible.

    • Restarts improve each run's quality; they do not create structure that is not there.

  5. the unusual split

    For anomaly detection, how should you split 10,000 normal examples and 20 known anomalies?

    • The model fits μ and σ to describe normal. Contaminating training with anomalies widens the distribution until the anomalies look normal, so every one is spent on evaluation instead.

    • That puts anomalies in the training set, which is precisely what you must not do.

    • That would be supervised learning. This method never learns what an anomaly looks like — it learns what normal looks like and flags everything else.

    • They are the only way to choose ε and the only way to measure whether the system works.

    What this is really testingTrain on normal only. Spend every anomaly on evaluation.

  6. which technique

    You have 6,000 examples of fraud and expect future fraud to look like past fraud. Anomaly detection or supervised learning?

    • The deciding question is not how many positives you have but whether future ones will resemble past ones. Here they will, so a classifier can learn the pattern.

    • Rarity alone does not decide it. With 6,000 recognisable examples a classifier will do better than density estimation.

    • You have labels. Throwing away 6,000 of them to avoid using them is not an advantage.

    • Both are used for fraud in practice, often together — a classifier for the known, density estimation for the novel.

    What this is really testingWill future positives resemble past ones? Yes → classifier. No → anomaly detection.

  7. feature transforms

    A feature in your anomaly detector is heavily right-skewed. What should you do?

    • The model assumes a Gaussian per feature. Making that assumption approximately true is usually the single highest-value step in the pipeline.

    • A skewed feature may be highly informative. Transform first, remove only if it earns nothing.

    • It does not. Fitting a Gaussian to a heavy-tailed feature mislabels ordinary large values as anomalies.

    • Scaling shifts and stretches; it does not change the shape. A skewed feature is still skewed afterwards.

  8. metrics

    Why is accuracy the wrong metric for evaluating an anomaly detector?

    • The same skewed-data trap as C2 W3. Precision, recall and F1 on the anomaly class are the metrics that mean something.

    • It can be measured — that is what the labelled anomalies in the CV and test sets are for.

    • It does change it, and that is not the reason accuracy is misleading.

    • It computes fine. It is just uninformative here.

  9. the independence assumption

    A server has normal CPU and normal traffic individually, but high CPU with low traffic. Why might the independent-Gaussian model miss it?

    • Each feature is individually unremarkable, and the product of two ordinary probabilities is ordinary. Catching this needs either the multivariate Gaussian or an engineered ratio feature.

    • Even with perfectly Gaussian features, the independence assumption is what loses the joint structure.

    • Lowering ε flags more things overall, including many genuinely normal ones. It does not recover the specific joint signal.

    • More data does not create a dependency the model has no way to represent.

    What this is really testingThe fix is a ratio feature — CPU divided by traffic — or a multivariate Gaussian.

  10. the normal distribution

    For a Gaussian with μ = 50 and σ = 5, roughly what fraction of values fall between 40 and 60?

    • 40 and 60 are two standard deviations either side of the mean. The 68 / 95 / 99.7 rule is worth memorising — it converts a threshold into an expected false-alarm rate.

    • That is one standard deviation: 45 to 55.

    • That is three standard deviations: 35 to 65.

    • 50% is the fraction below the mean, which is a different question.
