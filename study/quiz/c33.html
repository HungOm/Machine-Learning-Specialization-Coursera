C3 · Week 3 · 10 questions · pass mark 80%

Reinforcement Learning

Return, policy, Q(s,a), Bellman and the three refinements. Work the six-square rover by hand before sitting this — most of these are answerable from that strip.

Closed book. No notes, no scrolling back — that is the condition the real one is sat under.
—

  1. the return

    With rewards 100 at state 1, 40 at state 6, zero elsewhere and γ = 0.5, what is the return from state 4 if you always go left?

    • Three steps to reach state 1: 0 + 0 + 0 + 0.5³ × 100 = 0.125 × 100 = 12.5. The discount is applied once per step taken.

    • That is the undiscounted reward. γ < 1 means a distant reward is worth less than a near one.

    • That is one step of discounting, from state 2.

    • That is two steps of discounting, from state 3.

    What this is really testingAt γ = 0.5 a reward six steps away is worth about 1.6% of face value.

  2. policies

    What is a policy in reinforcement learning?

    • Not a plan or a sequence. A lookup that has an answer ready for whichever state you actually find yourself in, which is why RL survives surprises that break classical planning.

    • That is a plan, and it falls apart the moment something unexpected happens.

    • That is the return.

    • That is the environment's transition dynamics, not the agent's policy.

  3. the Q function

    What exactly does Q(s, a) mean?

    • The definition is deliberately strange — a may be foolish. That is what makes the comparison between two actions clean: any difference is caused by the first move alone.

    • That is R(s). Q looks at the whole discounted future, which is the entire point.

    • That is a stochastic policy. Q is a value, not a probability.

    • Acting optimally afterwards is what makes Q comparable across actions.

    What this is really testingOnce Q exists, the policy is free: stand in a state, read the numbers, take the largest.

  4. Bellmanselect all that apply

    The Bellman equation says Q(s,a) = R(s) + γ max Q(s′, a′). Which are true?

    • What I get now, plus the discounted best I can do from where I land. That recursion is Bellman's principle of optimality.

    • The right-hand side becomes a training target and the network's output is the prediction. That reframing is the whole trick behind DQN.

    • s′ is where you land, and a′ ranges over what you could do there.

    • It needs only the next state. That locality is exactly why it is useful.

    • For stochastic environments the right-hand side becomes an expectation. The structure is unchanged.

  5. explore vs exploit

    Why is an ε-greedy policy used during training?

    • You never take the action you underrate, so you never learn that you underrated it. The error is stable, and a small amount of forced randomness is what breaks it.

    • It often converges more slowly in the short term, because some steps are deliberately wasted. It converges to something better.

    • Memory is unaffected.

    • Negative Q values are perfectly normal when rewards are negative.

    What this is really testingε is normally decayed — near 1.0 early, about 0.01 later.

  6. function approximation

    Why does moving from six discrete states to a continuous state space require a neural network?

    • You need something that takes a state it has never seen and produces a value anyway — which is function approximation, and exactly what you spent two courses building.

    • A table lookup is far faster. The issue is that the table cannot exist.

    • Rewards are defined the same way regardless of how states are represented.

    • It applies unchanged. Only how you store Q changes.

    What this is really testingTabular Q-learning provably converges. With a network it does not — hence the three refinements.

  7. DQN architecture

    The improved DQN architecture outputs one Q value per action rather than taking the action as an input. Why?

    • A fourfold reduction in inference cost per decision, from a change to the last layer only — and training involves millions of decisions.

    • Accuracy is not the motivation; efficiency is.

    • The opposite: this architecture requires a fixed, finite, discrete action set.

    • Bellman still supplies the training targets.

  8. soft updates

    What problem does the soft update of the target network solve?

    • Chasing a target that runs away is why naive deep Q-learning oscillates or diverges. Moving the target slowly — 0.01 new, 0.99 old — makes it nearly stationary.

    • That is what the replay buffer's size limit does.

    • Exploration is ε-greedy's job.

    • Both exist and do different jobs — one governs the weight update, the other the target's drift.

  9. where RL actually worksselect all that apply

    Which are honest statements about the state of reinforcement learning?

    • Sample efficiency is the practical limitation, and it is why nearly every deployed success has either a simulator or a very cheap failure.

    • Agents optimise exactly what you wrote, including the parts you did not mean. Reward hacking is the standard outcome of a sloppy specification.

    • The reward comes from human preference comparisons rather than a simulator — one answer to the sample-efficiency problem rather than an exception to it.

    • The overwhelming majority of deployed machine learning is supervised, and comfortably so.

    • This is the hardest setting, because failure is expensive and a policy tuned in simulation often does not transfer.

  10. stochastic environments

    In a stochastic environment where the intended action succeeds 90% of the time, what changes?

    • You no longer choose where you end up; you choose a distribution over where you might. Steer for the average outcome being good.

    • It applies with an expectation on the right-hand side. The structure is intact.

    • Sometimes, and not always — it depends on the rewards. A large reward can be worth a 10% risk of misstep.

    • γ is a separate modelling choice about how far-sighted the agent should be.
