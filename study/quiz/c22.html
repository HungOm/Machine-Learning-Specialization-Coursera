C2 · Week 2 · 10 questions · pass mark 80%

Neural Network Training

Training in TensorFlow, activation functions, softmax and the numerical-stability flag. The activation questions separate the output layer from the hidden layers — so does the real quiz.

Closed book. No notes, no scrolling back — that is the condition the real one is sat under.
—

  1. the three steps

    What do the three steps Sequential, compile and fit correspond to?

    • Exactly the three objects you built by hand in C1. Every training script you write is these three, elaborated.

    • Data is passed to fit; prediction is a separate call afterwards.

    • Forward and backward passes both happen inside fit. They are not separate API steps.

    • Initialisation is automatic and regularisation is an argument to a layer, not one of the three calls.

    What this is really testingModel, loss, optimiser is the organising structure of every deep learning framework.

  2. hidden activations

    Which activation should you use in the hidden layers of most networks, and why?

    • The derivative is exactly 1 on the positive side, so gradients survive many layers of the chain rule. The sigmoid's derivative peaks at 0.25 and multiplying ten of those leaves almost nothing.

    • Probabilities are wanted at the output, not internally — and the sigmoid is exactly the function that causes vanishing gradients in hidden layers.

    • A stack of linear layers collapses to a single linear layer, so the network gains nothing from depth. This is the point of the “why do we need activations?” lesson.

    • Softmax couples all units in a layer and is for a multiclass output. Using it internally makes no sense.

  3. output activationsselect all that apply

    Match the output activation to the task. Which pairings are correct?

    • One output, squashed to (0, 1) — a probability for the positive class.

    • No activation at all. Anything else caps the range, which is why a sigmoid on a price model silently prevents predictions above 1.

    • Softmax couples the outputs so they sum to 1, which is what “exactly one” means.

    • This is the architecture bug the multi-label lesson warns about. Softmax forces the outputs to compete; use independent sigmoids instead.

    • Sigmoid caps at 1. ReLU is the right choice for a non-negative unbounded quantity.

    What this is really testingThe output activation follows from the range of y. The hidden one is ReLU by default.

  4. numerical stability

    What does from_logits=True do, and why does it matter?

    • Computing the probability and then its log loses precision. Done together, the intermediate never has to exist — which is what prevents exp overflow and log(0) producing NaN.

    • Label encoding is handled by which loss you choose — sparse or otherwise — not by this flag.

    • The softmax still happens; it happens inside the loss. The gain is numerical, not speed.

    • Regularization is set on layers via kernel_regularizer, and is unrelated.

    What this is really testingIf your loss goes to NaN a few hundred steps in, this is the first thing to check.

  5. softmax outputsselect all that apply

    Softmax outputs for a 4-class problem are [0.1, 0.6, 0.25, 0.05]. Which are true?

    • Softmax always normalises, which is what makes the outputs a distribution over the classes.

    • The argmax. 0.6 is the largest.

    • Softmax is a function of all logits at once. The outputs are coupled — which is exactly why it is wrong for multi-label problems.

    • Accuracy is measured over a dataset. This is one prediction's confidence, not a performance metric.

    • They cannot. That independence is what sigmoid outputs have and softmax outputs do not.

  6. why non-linearity

    Why does a network need non-linear activation functions at all?

    • A composition of linear functions is linear. Ten layers with no activation have exactly the expressive power of one, and all the depth is wasted.

    • Vanishing gradients are caused by certain activations, notably the sigmoid. Removing activations does not cause them.

    • It does not — activation=None is legal, and used deliberately for a linear output layer.

    • Some activations do that; ReLU does not, and it is the standard hidden choice.

  7. Adam

    What does the Adam optimiser do that plain gradient descent does not?

    • Parameters with consistently small gradients get larger effective steps and vice versa, so you spend far less time hand-tuning a single global α.

    • No optimiser guarantees that on a non-convex surface. Adam converges faster and more reliably; it does not change what minima exist.

    • You still supply an initial learning rate. Adam adapts around it.

    • It uses running averages of the gradient and its square — cheap first-order statistics, not a true Hessian.

  8. multi-label vs multiclass

    You are classifying images that may contain a car, a bus and a pedestrian, any combination. What should the output layer be?

    • Three independent yes/no questions. The outputs need not sum to 1, and each can be read on its own.

    • Softmax forces exactly one answer. The model would never report a car and a pedestrian together, no matter how much data you gave it — the architecture forbids it.

    • One unit answers one question. You have three.

    • This technically works and scales terribly — 2n classes — and it cannot share evidence between combinations that contain the same object.

    What this is really testingCan two answers be true at once? Yes → sigmoids. No → softmax.

  9. derivatives

    What is a derivative, in the sense used by gradient descent?

    • A rate of change — the slope of the tangent at that point. Steep slope, big step; flat slope, small step, which is why descent slows near a minimum without being told to.

    • That is the function itself. The derivative describes how it is changing there.

    • That is the integral — the opposite operation.

    • That is the error. The derivative of the cost happens to involve it, but they are not the same thing.

  10. backpropagation

    What does backpropagation on a computation graph actually do?

    • One backward walk over the same graph the forward pass built, attributing the loss one link at a time. It costs about the same as the forward pass, which is what makes training large networks affordable.

    • That is numerical differentiation. It is far more expensive and is used only to check an implementation.

    • It computes gradients. The optimiser then uses them to update — two separate steps.

    • The architecture is fixed before training. Searching over architectures is a different activity entirely.
