C2 · Week 3 · 10 questions · pass mark 80%

Advice for Applying ML

Bias, variance, baselines, learning curves and error metrics. This is the week the graded quiz leans on hardest, because every question is a diagnosis rather than a fact.

Closed book. No notes, no scrolling back — that is the condition the real one is sat under.
—

  1. diagnosing bias

    Training error 12%, cross-validation error 13%, human-level performance 3%. What is the problem, and what should you do?

    • The gap that matters is baseline→training: 9 points. Training→CV is only 1 point, so variance is not the issue. The model is not capable enough.

    • More data closes a training→CV gap, and that gap is 1 point. You would be spending months fixing a problem you do not have.

    • Increasing λ makes the model less flexible, which worsens a bias problem.

    • 12% against a human baseline of 3% is a four-fold error rate. There is substantial headroom.

    What this is really testingRead the two gaps, not the three numbers: baseline→training is bias, training→CV is variance.

  2. data splits

    Why is a three-way train / cross-validation / test split preferred over just train / test?

    • Try twenty models and report the best test score, and you have fitted the test set with twenty attempts. The number is no longer an unbiased estimate of new-data performance.

    • Three splits give you less training data, not more. The reason is about honesty of the estimate.

    • It is normally much smaller — often 20% or less.

    • Convergence is entirely unrelated to how you split the data.

    What this is really testingEvery decision you make on a set costs you the right to use it as an unbiased estimate.

  3. reading learning curvesselect all that apply

    Your learning curves show training and cross-validation error close together, both flat and both high, at the right-hand edge. Which are true?

    • Both errors are high and the gap is small. The model is not capable enough to fit the pattern.

    • The curves have already met and flattened. More data closes a gap, and there is no gap to close — this is the single most valuable thing a learning curve tells you.

    • More features, a higher polynomial degree, a bigger network, or less regularisation are the bias fixes.

    • That reduces flexibility further, making a bias problem worse.

    • Overfitting shows as a large persistent gap. Here there is barely any.

  4. skewed data

    A dataset is 99.5% negative. Your model predicts “negative” for everything. What are its accuracy, precision and recall?

    • It gets every negative right and every positive wrong. Recall is 0/all-positives = 0. Precision is 0/0 — it never made a positive prediction — which is why accuracy is useless on skewed data.

    • Recall counts the positives it found, and it found none.

    • Accuracy is the fraction of all predictions that are right, and 99.5% of them are.

    • This is the trap the whole lesson exists to prevent. Only accuracy is high, and it is high for a worthless model.

    What this is really testingOn skewed data, always report precision and recall. Accuracy hides a useless model.

  5. threshold

    You raise the classification threshold from 0.5 to 0.8. What happens?

    • You make fewer positive predictions and are more sure of each, so a higher share are correct — and you miss more of the true positives.

    • That is what lowering the threshold does.

    • They move in opposite directions. If a change improved both, you would have a better model, not a different operating point.

    • The model does not change at all. The threshold is applied to its output after training, which is why one trained model gives you a whole curve of operating points.

    What this is really testingWhich way you move it is a business decision about which error costs more.

  6. error analysis

    You have 100 misclassified emails. Error analysis shows 43 are pharmaceutical spam and 5 use deliberate misspellings. What follows?

    • Fixing the misspelling category perfectly buys 5% of your errors. Fixing the pharmaceutical category buys 43%. Same effort, eight times the return.

    • This is the most common way strong engineers waste time: solving the most interesting failure rather than the most common one.

    • Error analysis is an afternoon and tells you which data to collect. Doing it first is what makes the collection targeted.

    • The whole point of counting is that they do not. A tally exists to rank the work.

    What this is really testingManual, unglamorous, no library for it — and it routinely beats a week of tuning.

  7. data augmentationselect all that apply

    Which of these are valid data augmentations for a photo classifier?

    • A plausible thing that happens to real photographs, so it teaches the model something true about deployment.

    • Lighting genuinely varies. This is one of the most effective augmentations in practice.

    • Real cameras produce blurred photographs, especially handheld ones.

    • This distortion does not occur in deployment, and augmentations that do not reflect reality reliably fail to help. The test is always: would this really happen?

    • A mirrored 2 is not a 2. Mirroring is fine for cats and catastrophic here, which shows how domain-specific the choice is.

  8. transfer learning

    You have 500 labelled medical scans and want to use a network pre-trained on ImageNet. What is the sensible approach?

    • With very little data, unfreezing everything destroys the pre-trained weights. The early layers learned edges and textures, which are as true of scans as of photographs.

    • 500 examples is nowhere near enough. This discards the most valuable thing you have — someone else's 14 million images.

    • A high learning rate on 500 examples will wreck the pre-trained features in a few steps. This is the most common way transfer learning is misapplied.

    • Its output layer predicts ImageNet categories — dogs, boats. It has no notion of your classes.

  9. the NN recipe

    What is the recommended loop for a neural network when you have adequate compute and can obtain data?

    • Two questions and a loop. It works because a large, well-regularised network rarely does worse than a small one — which broke the classical bias–variance trade-off.

    • The loop starts small and grows. Starting enormous wastes compute and slows every iteration of the diagnosis.

    • Architecture comes first; λ is tuned once a model is big enough to overfit.

    • More data does not reduce training error — it usually increases it, because there is more to fit.

  10. fairnessselect all that apply

    A hiring model shows equal overall accuracy but a much higher false-negative rate for one group. Which are true?

    • One number over the whole population averages the groups together. The gap is invisible unless you split the metric, which is the entire technique.

    • Correlated features carry the same information. This is why simply dropping the column is not a solution, and why people who propose it are usually surprised.

    • The model learns the distribution of its training labels, including whatever produced them. If past decisions were biased, it reproduces the bias while looking objective.

    • Imbalance is a reason to check, not an excuse. Threshold adjustment, reweighting and constrained training all exist.

    • Equal aggregate accuracy with unequal error rates means the harms fall unevenly, which is exactly the failure being measured.

    What this is really testingSplit every metric by group. It is one line of code and it is the whole defence.
