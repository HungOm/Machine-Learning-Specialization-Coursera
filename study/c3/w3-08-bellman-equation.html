13.8The Bellman equation

One line that defines Q in terms of itself. Every reinforcement learning algorithm ever written is built on it.

C3 · Week 3 · lesson 8 of 16169 of 177 · 95% of the book

❓Before you read

Any long journey splits into “this step” plus “the rest”. Guess how that idea becomes an equation for Q.

I have committed to an answer

Watch for the two halves: what you get now, plus the discounted best you can do next.

🎈The idea, in plain words

In plain English

How much is today worth?

What you get today, plus how good tomorrow will be — slightly discounted, because tomorrow is not today.

That is the whole equation. Every long journey splits into “this step” plus “the rest of the journey”. And “the rest of the journey” is the same kind of question, one step smaller — so the same rule applies again.

🔧If you have never seen a formula

Asking directions and getting a useful answer: “take this turning, then ask again.”

Nobody hands you the whole route. Each person tells you one step and points at somebody who knows the rest. Do that repeatedly and you arrive, without anyone ever having known the entire journey.

🌉If you know a neighbouring field

This is the fundamental recursion of dynamic programming, and it is the same structure as the recurrence behind shortest paths or optimal stopping.

The insight, and it is Bellman's, is the principle of optimality: an optimal path's tail is itself optimal. That is what licenses breaking a long-horizon problem into one step plus a smaller version of the same problem.

👁One thing to picture

Q(s,a) = R(s) + γ max Q(s′,a′), written once by hand.

Two pieces only: what I get now, and the discounted best I can do from where I land. Every reinforcement learning algorithm you will ever meet is a way of making this equation true.

⚖️Where this really costs something

The equation is what turns RL into something a network can be trained on. The right-hand side is a target, the left is a prediction, and the difference between them is a supervised learning problem you already know how to solve.

That reframing — treat the Bellman right-hand side as a label — is precisely what lesson 12 does, and it is the whole trick behind DQN.

So the equation below is not a formula to memorise. It is “one step, then the same problem again”.

The one thing to remember

Two halves: what you get now, plus the discounted value of the best thing you can do next. Every RL algorithm is a different strategy for making an estimate of Q satisfy this equation.

🎬Watch it move

▶ Pick a state and an action, and watch the equation assembleeach coloured box is one term

🧮Worked, on state 4

Q(4, ←): from state 4, going left lands you in state 3.

  • R(4) = 0 — nothing collected right now.
  • The best you can do from state 3 is V(3) = max(25, 6.25) = 25.
  • So Q(4, ←) = 0 + 0.5 × 25 = 12.5. ✓

At a terminal state there is no “next”, so the second term vanishes and Q(s, a) = R(s). That is the base case that stops the recursion — the same structural role as the leaf in a decision tree.

Say it out loud

Q(4, ←) came out as 0 + 0.5 × 25. Why is the whole future compressed into that single number 25?

said it — now check

Because V(3) already is the whole future from state 3, played optimally. The recursion means you never look further than one step: everything beyond is summarised in the value of where you land.

🔬Why this makes the circular definition work

Lesson 6 defined Q in terms of “behaving optimally afterwards”, which sounds circular. The Bellman equation turns that circularity into something computable: it is a system of equations that Q must satisfy, and it has exactly one solution.

You can find it by starting with any guess and repeatedly applying the equation as an update — each application brings you closer, guaranteed. That procedure is value iteration, and it is precisely what computes the numbers in every demo on this page.

Where the name comes from

Richard Bellman introduced this in the 1950s while working on dynamic programming — a name he reportedly chose because it sounded impressive to a research-funding department that was hostile to mathematics. The technique long predates machine learning and shows up everywhere from route planning to sequence alignment.

🕳Traps

Common trap

Forgetting the max. It is max over the next action a′, not over a. The action you are evaluating is fixed; the future one is optimised.

Common trap

Using R(s′) instead of R(s). In this course’s convention the reward belongs to the state you are leaving. Other texts use R(s, a, s′). Both are internally consistent; mixing them is not.

✅Check yourself

Compute Q(2, →) with γ = 0.5.

Going right from 2 lands in state 3, where V(3) = 25. So Q(2, →) = 0 + 0.5 × 25 = 12.5.

What is Q(s, a) at a terminal state?

Just R(s). There is no next state, so the second term disappears.

Why is the Bellman equation recursive, and why does that not make it circular?

Q appears on both sides, so it is defined in terms of itself. It is not circular because the equation has a unique solution, reachable by repeated application from any starting guess — value iteration.

  3. From quiz question 4 · Bellman

    Spot the errorThis working looks right and is not. Find the exact line that fails before you open the answer — this is the skill a quiz distractor tests.

    Three stored experiences. q_next holds Q(s′, a′) for both actions in each experience's next state:

    q_next = np.array([[3.0, 7.0], [12.0, 4.0], [0.5, 1.0]])
r = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 0.0])
gamma = 0.5
y = r + gamma * np.max(q_next)

    The targets come out as [6, 7, 6]. Which line is wrong, and what should the targets be?

    show me where it breaks

    The last line. np.max(q_next) takes one max over the whole batch — 12, from the second experience — and gives it to all three. The max must be over the actions of each next state: np.max(q_next, axis=1) = [7, 12, 1].

    Correct targets: y = [3.5, 7, 0.5]. The first and third experiences were being credited with a future they will never see.

    Say it three ways
    In one line

    The max in Bellman is over the next state's actions, one row at a time.

    Like you are ten

    Each child should get the best prize from their own lucky bag, not the best prize in anybody's bag.

    The words, in simple words
    mini-batcha handful of stored experiences trained on together
    axis=1work along each row separately
