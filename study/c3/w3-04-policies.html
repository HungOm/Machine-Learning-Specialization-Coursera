13.4Making decisions: policies

A policy is a lookup table: state in, action out. Finding the best one is the entire problem.

C3 · Week 3 · lesson 4 of 16165 of 177 · 93% of the book

❓Before you read

You want a rule that says what to do in every state. Guess what that object is called, and what makes one such rule better than another.

I have committed to an answer

Watch for π, which here has nothing to do with 3.14159.

🎈The idea, in plain words

In plain English

A policy is your plan for every situation, written down in advance.

“On square 2, go left. On square 3, go left. On square 4, go left. On square 5, go right.”

That is it. That is a policy. It is not a sequence of moves — it is a rule that tells you what to do wherever you find yourself.

🔧If you have never seen a formula

A ward round protocol. Not a plan for one patient — a rule that says, for any patient in any state you might find them in, what you do next.

The distinction is the whole lesson. A plan is a sequence and it falls apart the moment something unexpected happens. A protocol still answers, because it is a lookup on the situation you are actually in.

🌉If you know a neighbouring field

Formally a policy π is a function from state to action, and that type signature is worth holding on to.

Anyone who has written an event handler or a control law has written a policy: not a script, but a rule that is ready for whichever input arrives. It is the closed-loop version of a plan.

👁One thing to picture

A small table. Left column: every state. Right column: the action to take there.

Six rows for the rover. That table is the policy — the entire output of reinforcement learning, the thing everything else in the week is machinery for producing.

⚖️Where this really costs something

Because a policy is defined for every state, it does not need replanning when the world surprises it — which is exactly why RL is used for control problems rather than classical planning.

A robot that has slipped is simply in a different state, and the policy has an answer for that state already.

V(s)“the value of state s”The expected return if you start in s and follow π from then on.
The one thing to remember

The goal, stated precisely: find the policy π that maximises the expected return from every state. Remarkably, in an MDP there is always a single policy that is optimal for all states simultaneously — you never have to trade one state off against another.

🔬The optimal policy here is not uniform

For the rover with γ = 0.5, the optimal policy is ← ← ← →: states 2, 3 and 4 go left towards the 100, but state 5 goes right towards the 40.

That split is the interesting part. State 5 is one step from a reward of 40 and four steps from a reward of 100, and with γ = 0.5 that distance is decisive. The dividing line sits between states 4 and 5, and changing γ moves it.

🕳Traps

Common trap

Thinking of a policy as a route. It is not “go left, left, left”. It is a rule covering every square, including ones you might never visit. That matters the moment the environment is stochastic and you end up somewhere unplanned.

Say it out loud

A policy is a rule covering every square, including ones you may never visit. Say when that apparently wasteful completeness starts to matter.

said it — now check

The moment the environment is stochastic. If an action succeeds only 90% of the time, you will sometimes end up in a square your plan never intended to reach — and a route has nothing to say there.

A policy has an answer for that square already, so it simply carries on. That is the difference between a plan and a rule, and it is the reason RL is used for control problems rather than classical planning.

✅Check yourself

Write a policy for the rover as a table.

π(2) = ←, π(3) = ←, π(4) = ←, π(5) = →. States 1 and 6 are terminal, so no action is needed there.

Why does the optimal policy send state 5 right when states 2–4 go left?

Because state 5 is one step from 40 and four steps from 100. With γ = 0.5, 0.5×40 = 20 beats 0.5⁴×100 = 6.25.

Does an optimal policy always exist for a finite MDP?

Yes — and it can always be taken to be deterministic and optimal for every state at once. This is a genuine theorem, not just a convenient assumption.

    Spot the errorThis working looks right and is not. Find the exact line that fails before you open the answer — this is the skill a quiz distractor tests.

    A team writes down what their rover should do from state 4:

    1. The best route from 4 is left, left, left.
    2. So we store the policy as the list [←, ←, ←] and play it in order.
    3. On the first move the wheels slip and the rover lands in state 5.
    4. It plays the remaining two moves, ← ←, and stops in state 3.

    Which line is the mistake?

    show me where it breaks

    Line 2. A list is a plan, not a policy. After the slip it keeps replaying moves made for states it is no longer in, and runs out of moves in state 3 with no reward at all.

    A policy is looked up from the state you are in. In state 5 the optimal policy says →, worth V(5) = 20; it never needed the slip to be predicted.

    Say it three ways
    In one line

    Store one action per state, so a surprise leaves you with an answer.

    Like you are ten

    Directions that only say “left, left, left” are useless once you take a wrong turn. A map that tells you what to do from every corner still works.

  4. From quiz question 2 · policies

    TransferA situation the lesson never showed. If the idea only works on the lesson's own example, you have memorised the example, not the idea.

    A robot moves on a 4 × 4 grid with 4 moves (up, down, left, right). Two corner squares end the episode. How many entries does a policy need, and how many different policies are there? Repeat for a 10 × 10 grid. What does that tell you about finding the best policy?

    compare with my answer

    4 × 4: 14 non-terminal squares, so a policy is 14 entries, and there are 414 = 268,435,456 different policies. 10 × 10: 98 entries and about 1 × 1059 policies.

    Nobody finds the best policy by trying them all. You compute Q (or estimate it with a network) and read the policy off with an argmax, one state at a time. The rover's 16 policies were small enough to check by brute force; nothing real is.

    Say it three ways
    In one line

    A policy is a small table, but the number of possible tables explodes.

    Like you are ten
