13.11The lunar lander

The assignment problem, stated fully. Eight state numbers, four actions, and a reward function that is doing more work than it looks.

C3 · Week 3 · lesson 11 of 16172 of 177 · 97% of the book

❓Before you read

Eight numbers in, four possible actions. Guess how you would design the reward so the lander learns to land rather than to hover forever.

I have committed to an answer

Watch for how much of RL is reward design, and how easily a plausible reward produces absurd behaviour.

🎈The idea, in plain words

In plain English

Land a little spaceship gently on a landing pad. You have three thrusters: one pointing down, one pointing left, one pointing right. You can also do nothing and just fall.

Land softly on the pad: big prize. Crash: big penalty. Every puff of the engine costs a tiny bit, because fuel is not free.

Nobody tells you the correct thruster to fire at any moment. You just try, crash a few thousand times, and gradually work it out.

🔧If you have never seen a formula

A landing you have to get right: slow enough not to break, upright, between the flags, without running out of fuel.

Nobody can write down the rule for the throttle. But everybody can say afterwards whether the landing was good, and that is a much weaker requirement — it is the only one RL needs.

🌉If you know a neighbouring field

The reward function is the specification, and writing one is genuinely hard engineering.

If you have designed a KPI or an incentive scheme, you know the failure: agents optimise exactly what you wrote, including the parts you did not mean. Reward shaping is that problem with a fast, tireless adversary.

👁One thing to picture

A lander, eight state numbers, four possible actions — nothing, left thruster, main, right — and a reward with several terms added together.

Note that the reward includes both a landing bonus and a per-frame fuel penalty. Those two terms pulling against each other are what produce a controlled descent rather than a hover or a drop.

⚖️Where this really costs something

Reward hacking is not a curiosity; it is the standard outcome of a sloppy reward. Agents have learned to spin in circles for a proximity bonus, and to crash quickly when a per-step penalty made living expensive.

The discipline that helps is the same as error analysis in C2 W3: watch the failures, and fix the specification rather than the model.

So the reward function below is the actual design work. The algorithm is the easy half.

🎯The reward function, which is the real design work

EventReward
getting to the landing pad+100 to +140, depending on how close
crashing−100

firing the main engine−0.3 per step
firing a side thruster−0.03 per step
The one thing to remember

Look at what that reward function is quietly encoding: get there, do not crash, land level, and do not waste fuel — four competing objectives, balanced by their relative magnitudes. Writing this is far more of an art than writing the algorithm, and it is where most of the real engineering time goes.

🔬Why the numbers are the sizes they are

The fuel penalties are deliberately tiny (−0.03, −0.3) compared to the landing rewards (±100). If fuel cost too much, the optimal policy would be to switch everything off and drop like a stone — crashing is only −100, and hovering for a hundred steps costs −30.

Make fuel cheaper still and the lander hovers indefinitely, burning fuel while never quite committing to landing, because landing risks the −100. The balance between these numbers is the specification of the behaviour, and small changes produce qualitatively different pilots.

🔤The words, decoded

SymbolSay it out loudWhat it actually is

action space“the actions”What the agent may do. Here 4: nothing, left thruster, main, right.
episode“one attempt”One run from reset to crash or landing.
reward function“the reward”The numbers that define success. Designing it is the real work, and it is where things go wrong.
sparse reward“sparse”Reward arriving only at the very end, so the agent gets almost no signal along the way.

🕳Traps

Common trap

Forgetting the leg-contact flags are binary. They are 0 or 1 while everything else is a real number. Feeding raw unnormalised state into a network mixes scales — the C2 W1 scaling lesson applies.

Say it out loud

The two leg-contact flags are 0 or 1 while the other six state values are real numbers. Say why that matters, using the C2 Week 1 lesson.

said it — now check

Mixed scales. If velocity runs to ±5 and position to ±1.5 while the flags are 0 or 1, the network's first layer sees inputs of wildly different magnitude, and the large ones dominate the weighted sum before any learning happens.

It is exactly the feature-scaling problem from C1 Week 2 and C2 Week 1, arriving in a new costume. Nothing about reinforcement learning exempts you from it — the network in the middle is an ordinary network with ordinary requirements.

✅Check yourself

Why include velocities in the state and not just positions?

Because of the Markov property. Position alone cannot tell you whether the lander is descending gently or plummeting — and the correct action differs completely.

Why is the fuel penalty so much smaller than the crash penalty?

So that saving fuel never becomes worth risking a crash. The relative sizes encode the priority ordering of the objectives.

What would happen if you removed the fuel penalty entirely?

The lander would have no incentive to be efficient — it might hover indefinitely, or fire everything constantly. The penalty is what makes it commit to landing.
