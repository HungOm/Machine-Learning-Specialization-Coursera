13.16The state of reinforcement learning

An honest closing assessment — and the end of the specialization.

❓Before you read

RL produces spectacular demos. Guess how much of deployed machine learning actually uses it, and why.

I have committed to an answer

Watch for the honest assessment: the gap between simulation and the physical world is the recurring obstacle.

🎈The idea, in plain words

In plain English

Reinforcement learning is genuinely exciting, and it is also the most over-hyped part of machine learning right now.

In a video game or a simulator, it is astonishing — it can beat the best humans in the world. In the real world, on real robots, it is fragile, expensive, and much harder than the demos suggest.

Worth knowing. Not usually the first tool to reach for.

🔧If you have never seen a formula

A tool that does one thing spectacularly and is awkward everywhere else. Worth knowing exactly what it is for, and worth not carrying to every job.

The honest summary is that RL is transformative in simulation and difficult in the physical world, and knowing which side of that line you are on saves entire projects.

🌉If you know a neighbouring field

The practical limitation is sample efficiency. RL needs millions of episodes, and it learns by failing repeatedly.

That is free in a simulator and expensive or unacceptable with real hardware, real customers or real patients — which is why nearly every deployed success either has a simulator or has a very cheap failure.

👁One thing to picture

Two columns. Games, simulated robotics, chip layout, datacentre cooling, RLHF for language models on one side. Physical robots, healthcare, anything where a mistake is expensive on the other.

The dividing line is not difficulty. It is the cost of a failed attempt.

⚖️Where this really costs something

The genuinely large commercial application right now is RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback — which is how language models are turned from text predictors into assistants.

Note the shape: the reward comes from human comparisons rather than a simulator, which is one answer to the sample-efficiency problem rather than an exception to it.

So the assessment below is worth taking at face value. The technique is real, the hype is ahead of the deployments, and knowing the gap is professionally useful.

⚖️The honest assessment

What is real

  • Superhuman play in games with perfect simulators.
  • Genuine wins in control: data-centre cooling, some robotics.
  • RLHF — how essentially every modern chat model is tuned. Quietly the biggest deployed application of RL that exists.

What is hard

  • Simulation-to-reality transfer. The simulator is never the world.
  • Sample efficiency — millions of trials, which is fine in sim and impossible with a real drone.
  • Extreme sensitivity to reward design and hyperparameters.
  • Far fewer production applications than supervised learning.
The one thing to remember

Andrew’s own framing, and it is worth copying: the ideas are important and the research is exciting, and the number of commercial applications today is much smaller than the attention suggests. Being able to say that clearly is a mark of understanding the field rather than its marketing.

✅Check yourself

Why does RL work so much better in simulation than on real robots?

It needs millions of trials. In simulation those are free and fast; on real hardware they are slow, expensive, and each failure may break something. And a policy trained in simulation often fails on the real system because the simulator was subtly wrong.

What is the most widely deployed application of RL today?

RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback, used to align large language models. Not a robot in sight.
